Does God Exist? The Evidence for His Existence

The person who searches this question is rarely asking it as an abstract philosophical exercise. They are asking it from somewhere specific — from a season of doubt, from a conversation that shook what they thought they believed, from the honest admission that they want faith to be true and are not sure it is. "I want to believe but I need a reason" is the prayer underneath the search query.

This article takes that honestly. It does not pretend the question is simple. And it does not offer faith as a substitute for evidence — because the God of the Bible does not ask for blind faith. He asks for trust grounded in what can be known.

The Question Is Older Than You Are

The question "does God exist?" has been asked by serious thinkers for as long as serious thinking has occurred. The fact that it is still being asked does not mean it has not been answered. It means the answer requires more than a Wikipedia search — it requires genuine engagement with evidence that points in a specific direction.

There are three classical arguments for God's existence that philosophers and theologians have refined over centuries. Each addresses a different dimension of the question. None of them alone is decisive. Together they build a cumulative case that the most reasonable explanation of the world we inhabit is a God who made it.

The Cosmological Argument — Something From Nothing

The universe exists. This is where the argument begins — not with a theological premise but with the most basic observable fact: something is here rather than nothing.

Everything that exists has a cause. The chair was built. The tree grew from a seed. The planet formed from cosmic material. The pattern of causality is universal and unbroken in our observable experience: every effect has a cause prior to itself.

The universe — the totality of all physical reality, including space, time, matter, and energy — began to exist approximately 13.8 billion years ago at the Big Bang. This is not a theological claim. It is the scientific consensus, supported by the expansion of the universe traced backward in time, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and multiple independent lines of evidence.

If the universe began to exist, and everything that begins to exist has a cause, then the universe has a cause. And that cause must be outside the universe — outside space, outside time, outside matter and energy, because those things came into existence at the Big Bang. Whatever caused the universe is timeless, spaceless, immaterial, and immensely powerful.

The cosmological argument does not prove the God of the Bible. But it establishes that a cause outside the physical universe is more reasonable than the alternative — that the universe caused itself or appeared from nothing by no cause at all. Nothing produces nothing. The existence of something requires explanation.

The Teleological Argument — The Fine-Tuned Universe

The cosmological argument asks why anything exists. The teleological argument asks why what exists is the way it is.

Physicists have discovered that the fundamental constants of the universe — the strength of gravity, the mass of electrons, the rate of expansion after the Big Bang, the ratio of matter to antimatter — are calibrated with extraordinary precision for the existence of life. Change any one of them by a tiny fraction and the universe would not contain stars, planets, chemistry, or life.

The strength of gravity, for example, is calibrated to one part in 10 to the 60th power — a number so large it exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe. If gravity were slightly stronger, all matter would collapse. Slightly weaker, and no structures would form. The precision is staggering across dozens of independent constants.

Physicist Paul Davies wrote that the evidence for design is "overwhelming." This is not a religious statement. Davies is not a conventional theist. It is the scientific observation that the universe looks, with remarkable consistency, like something that was set up rather than something that randomly happened.

The multiverse hypothesis — the proposal that our universe is one of an infinite number of universes with varying constants, and we are in the one that permits life by chance — is the alternative proposed by some physicists. It is philosophically interesting. But it is currently untestable and unfalsifiable — which means it is not a scientific answer. It is a philosophical one. And a God who designed the universe is at least as coherent an explanation, and significantly more parsimonious.

The Moral Argument — The Standard That Exists Without Us

The third argument begins with an observation about human experience: we have genuine moral convictions. Not preferences or cultural customs — genuine convictions about right and wrong that we treat as objectively true.

When someone commits genocide, we do not say "that is not to my taste." We say it was wrong — not wrong for some people, not wrong in some cultures, genuinely wrong in a way that transcends the opinion of any individual or society. The Nuremberg trials convicted the Nazi war criminals not for violating German law (which they had not) but for violating a law above German law — a moral standard that all people everywhere are subject to regardless of what their government permits.

If objective moral standards exist — if some things are genuinely wrong regardless of opinion — then those standards require a ground. They cannot be grounded in evolution (natural selection selects for survival, not for moral truth). They cannot be grounded in social contract (contracts are preferences, not moral absolutes). They require a standard that is itself objective, personal, and moral — which is precisely what the God of the Bible is.

C.S. Lewis made this argument from his own experience as an atheist: his strongest argument against God was the apparent injustice of the universe. But he noticed that the concept of injustice required a standard of justice — and that the standard could not come from the unjust universe he was complaining about. The very argument he used against God was evidence for God.

The Resurrection — Where the Abstract Becomes Personal

The three arguments above build a cumulative case that God exists. But they describe a God who is impersonal — a first cause, a designer, a moral lawgiver. The most significant evidence for the God of the Bible is different in kind from these philosophical arguments. It is historical.

If Jesus rose from the dead — and the previous article in this series examines that evidence in detail — then God is not merely a philosophical inference. He is a person who entered history, who has a specific character, who makes specific claims, and who demonstrated his existence in the most public way available: by raising a dead man and letting five hundred people see him.

The resurrection does for the existence of God what no philosophical argument can: it makes God specific and personal. The God whose existence is inferred from cosmology is any god — an impersonal first cause who may or may not care about the creatures he made. The God who raised Jesus from the dead is the Father of Jesus Christ — the God who loved the world enough to send his only Son, who raises the dead, who has spoken in history, and who invites personal relationship.

Acts 17:30-31 — Paul in Athens, addressing the philosophers — did not argue from cosmology or teleology. He announced: "he has set a day when he will judge the world with justice by the man he has appointed. He has given proof of this to everyone by raising him from the dead." The proof that Paul offered to the philosophers of Athens was not a philosophical argument. It was a historical event — the resurrection — which established the God who performed it as the God of the entire human story.

What Faith Actually Is

The word "faith" has been distorted in popular usage to mean believing something without evidence — or even against evidence. This is not the biblical meaning of the word.

Hebrews 11:1 — "faith is confidence in what we hope for and assurance about what we do not see." The assurance is grounded in what is known — the character of the one who has promised, the evidence of what he has already done — and extends to what is not yet seen. It is the faith of someone who trusts a person on the basis of what they know about them, not someone who is believing in the dark.

The God of the Bible does not ask for blind faith. He provides evidence — in creation, in history, in the resurrection, in the testimony of Scripture, in the personal experience of those who have genuinely encountered him. Faith is the appropriate response to that evidence — not the absence of evidence, but the trusting reception of what the evidence points toward.

Psalm 19:1 — "The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of his hands." The declaration is there. The question is whether we are listening.
