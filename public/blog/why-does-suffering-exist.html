Why Does God Allow Suffering? An Honest Answer

By Sir Henry M M  ·  In Him Daily

Why Does God Allow Suffering? An Honest Answer

This article will not give you an easy answer. Not because one is unavailable but because the question does not deserve one. If you are asking "why does God allow suffering?" from a place of genuine pain — from a loss that still has not made sense, a prayer that still has not been answered, a thing that happened that you believe God could have stopped and did not — then you deserve honesty, not comfort that comes too quickly.

The Bible's answer to suffering is not a philosophical argument that resolves the problem. It is a person who entered it.

The Question That Will Not Go Away

The philosophical problem of suffering — sometimes called the problem of evil — goes like this: if God is all-good, he would want to prevent suffering. If God is all-powerful, he could prevent suffering. But suffering exists. Therefore either God is not all-good, or he is not all-powerful, or he does not exist.

This is a serious argument. It has caused serious people to abandon faith, and it deserves a serious response. But before the theological response, the human response: if you have lost someone, if you have experienced something that should not have happened, if your body has betrayed you or someone you love has been destroyed — the philosophical argument is not the thing you are actually asking about. You are asking about your specific pain. And that question is not solved by an argument. It is met by a presence.

The Bible knows this. It does not begin its response to suffering with an explanation. It begins with a lament.

The Lament Tradition — God Can Handle Your Anger

Psalm 22:1 — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, so far from my cries of anguish?" This is not the prayer of an unbeliever. It is the prayer of someone in genuine relationship with God, crying out from a place of experienced abandonment.

The Psalms contain more lament than any other genre of prayer — approximately a third of the Psalter is lament, complaint, and grief directed at God. Psalm 13 — "How long, Lord? Will you forget me forever?" Psalm 88 ends without resolution: "Darkness is my closest friend." Job spends thirty-five chapters demanding that God answer for what has happened to him. Jeremiah 20 contains one of the most raw and unguarded complaints in the entire Bible.

What the lament tradition of Scripture establishes is this: God can handle your anger. He does not require you to pretend that what happened was fine, or to arrive at theological resolution before you are allowed to come to him, or to suppress the grief and the questions. The lament is itself a form of faith — the cry of someone who still believes God exists and still believes he can hear, even when the hearing has not yet produced what was asked for.

If you are in that place — bring it. The tradition of Scripture does not just permit this. It models it.

What the Bible Does Not Say

There are several answers to suffering that the Bible does not give, and which should be refused when they are offered.

The Bible does not say that all suffering is the direct consequence of specific sin. Job's friends made this argument with great theological confidence and were explicitly rebuked by God (Job 42:7 — "you have not spoken the truth about me, as my servant Job has"). Jesus addressed the same assumption in John 9:2-3: "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" Jesus answered, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned." The automatic equation of suffering with personal sin is not biblical. It is cruel.

The Bible does not say that God causes all suffering for a specific purpose that will be revealed to you if you are spiritually mature enough. Romans 8:28 — "all things work together for good for those who love God" — does not mean that all things are good, or that God caused all things, or that the good will be visible to you within your lifetime. It is a declaration of cosmic confidence in divine sovereignty, not an explanation of individual suffering.

The Bible does not say that sufficient faith will prevent suffering or that suffering is evidence of insufficient faith. Paul, who suffered more than most (2 Corinthians 11:23-28 lists the catalogue), had prayed for the removal of his thorn in the flesh and been told no. His faith was not the problem. God said: "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9).

God Entered the Suffering — The Cross as Answer

The most important thing the Bible says about suffering is not an explanation. It is an event.

John 11:35 — "Jesus wept." The shortest verse in the English Bible contains the most significant theological claim about suffering in the New Testament. The Son of God, standing at the tomb of Lazarus — a tomb he was about to empty — wept. Not because he did not know what was coming. But because death is genuinely terrible. Because grief is genuinely real. Because the world as it is is not the world as it should be, and the one who made the world knows that more than anyone.

The cross is the place where God's response to suffering becomes definitive. Not: God explained suffering. Not: God removed suffering before its time. God entered it — fully, without reservation, without the protection that divine power could have provided. Philippians 2:6-8 — he "did not consider equality with God something to be used to his own advantage; rather, he made himself nothing... he humbled himself by becoming obedient to death — even death on a cross."

Hebrews 4:15 — "we do not have a high priest who is unable to empathise with our weaknesses, but we have one who has been tempted in every way, just as we are — yet he did not sin." The high priest who intercedes for us is not interceding from a position of untouched divine comfort. He is interceding from a position of genuine, experienced, bodily suffering — including the suffering of abandonment: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). Jesus quoted Psalm 22 from the cross. The lament of the psalmist became his own.

This does not explain why God allows suffering. But it does establish something that arguments alone cannot: the God who asks you to trust him through suffering has himself suffered. He is not asking you to go somewhere he has not been.

Romans 8:18-28 — The Groaning Creation

Romans 8:18-28 contains the New Testament's most sustained treatment of suffering — and it is remarkable for what it includes.

"The creation itself will be liberated from its bondage to decay" (8:21). Suffering is not the intended final state of creation. It is a condition from which creation will be freed — the world groaning toward a restoration that is coming.

"We ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for our adoption to sonship, the redemption of our bodies" (8:23). Believers groan too. The possession of the Spirit is not the solution to suffering — it is the down payment that makes the groaning bearable, because the down payment is the guarantee of what is coming.

"The Spirit himself intercedes for us through wordless groans" (8:26). Even the Spirit groans. The suffering of the present moment reaches into the inner life of the Trinity itself — the Spirit carrying what we cannot articulate toward the Father who already knows.

And then verse 28 — not as a conclusion that explains everything, but as a declaration that holds when nothing else does: "And we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him, who have been called according to his purpose." In all things — including the things that make no sense, the losses that should not have happened, the prayers that have not been answered. Not: all things are good. In all things God works for good. The working is active, ongoing, unseen from within the suffering, and guaranteed by the character of the one doing the working.

The Promise That Does Not Explain But Sustains

2 Corinthians 4:17 — "For our light and momentary troubles are achieving for us an eternal glory that far outweighs them all."

Paul wrote this while in the middle of a suffering catalogue that included shipwrecks, beatings, imprisonments, and the daily pressure of all the churches (2 Corinthians 11). He called these "light and momentary troubles" not because they were easy — they were not — but because of the weight of glory he was comparing them to. The comparison is not between bad things and good feelings. It is between temporal suffering and eternal glory. The disproportion is so vast that even the severest suffering recedes against it.

This does not resolve the pain. It does not answer the specific question of why. But it gives the suffering a frame that is large enough to hold it — a future so disproportionate to the present that the present, as terrible as it is, is not the final word.

Revelation 21:4 — "He will wipe every tear from their eyes." Not: he will explain every tear. He will wipe every one. The intimacy of the image — the personal wiping — is the final answer: not an argument but a presence, not an explanation but a restoration.
