REPENTANCE

What Is Repentance? More Than Feeling Sorry

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

Here is one of the most common experiences in the Christian life: you feel genuinely sorry for a sin. You confess it. You feel the weight of it lift briefly. And then, days or weeks later, you are back in the same place — feeling sorry again, confessing again, cycling through the same guilt and temporary relief without anything fundamentally changing.

If this is your experience, you are not alone. And the reason it is so common is that what most people call repentance is not what the Bible means by it.

Feeling sorry is not repentance. Feeling sorry is the emotional response to guilt — and it can exist completely independently of any genuine change of direction. Paul explicitly distinguishes between the two in 2 Corinthians 7:10: "Godly sorrow brings repentance that leads to salvation and leaves no regret, but worldly sorrow brings death." Sorrow is not repentance. Sorrow can lead to repentance — or it can lead to death. The difference depends on what happens next.

What the Word Actually Means

The Greek word behind "repentance" in the New Testament is metanoia. It is a compound word: meta (change, beyond) + noia (mind, understanding). Literally: a change of mind, a new way of thinking, a reorientation of perspective.

The word does not primarily describe an emotion. It describes a direction — a turning of the whole person toward a new orientation. It is the word for a ship changing course, an army reversing direction, a person who has been walking one way who turns and walks another.

This is why repentance in the New Testament is always paired with something else. It is never just turning away from something. It is always turning toward something — specifically, toward Christ. Acts 3:19 — "Repent, then, and turn to God, so that your sins may be wiped out, that times of refreshing may come from the Lord." The turning and the turning to God are the same movement. Repentance is not simply the abandonment of sin. It is the embrace of Christ.

This distinction changes everything. If repentance is only "stop doing bad things," then the focus is on the behaviour being left behind — and the person attempting it is constantly looking backward at what they are walking away from. But if repentance is "turn toward Christ," then the focus is on the one being turned toward — and the behaviour left behind is a consequence of the turning, not its mechanism.

The Guilt Cycle — What Repentance Without Turning Produces

The most common counterfeit of repentance is what we might call the guilt cycle: sin, feel sorry, confess, feel relief, repeat. The person in the guilt cycle experiences genuine emotional distress about their sin. They may cry. They may feel deep shame. They may make promises about the future. And then the cycle repeats.

The guilt cycle is not repentance — because the direction of the person's life has not fundamentally changed. They are still oriented the same way. They have added guilt and confession to a life pointed in the wrong direction. The guilt may even become its own form of spiritual performance: "I feel sorry enough that God will accept me again."

Paul's word for this pattern in 2 Corinthians 7:10 is "worldly sorrow" — sorrow that focuses on the consequences of sin, on the damage done, on the disappointment of self and others. Worldly sorrow produces shame spirals, self-condemnation, and the exhausting effort of trying to feel bad enough to deserve forgiveness. It does not produce transformation because it is still fundamentally about self — self-assessment, self-condemnation, self-improvement — rather than about Christ.

2 Corinthians 7:10 says worldly sorrow brings death. Not a dramatic sudden death — but the slow death of a spiritual life stuck in guilt, never arriving at the freedom that genuine repentance produces.

What Genuine Repentance Looks Like

Genuine repentance has a different texture entirely. Luke 15 contains three parables about the return of the lost — and the parable of the prodigal son gives the most detailed portrait of what genuine repentance looks like from the inside.

The son "came to himself" (Luke 15:17) — the first movement of repentance is a new clarity of vision, a moment of seeing your own situation honestly. He saw where he was, what he had done, and what was available to him if he turned. He did not wallow in the seeing. He acted on it.

"I will set out and go back to my father" (Luke 15:18) — repentance is the decision to move, not just the feeling of remorse. He got up and went. The decision preceded the arrival. He did not wait until he felt adequately sorry. He turned and walked.

"While he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son" (Luke 15:20) — and here is the most important thing about genuine repentance: it is met. The father runs before the son has completed the journey, before he has rehearsed his speech, before he has made any restitution. The turning itself — not the completion of the journey, not the achievement of sufficient contrition — is what the father runs toward.

This is the shape of genuine repentance: honest self-assessment, the decision to turn, the first step in the right direction — and the discovery that the Father was already watching and runs to meet you before you finish crossing the distance.

Repentance and the New Covenant

Under the Old Covenant, repentance was largely behavioural — returning to the law, restoring what was taken, making the prescribed offerings. The prophets sometimes anticipated something deeper: Jeremiah 4:4 — "circumcise your hearts" — and Ezekiel 18:31 — "get a new heart and a new spirit." But the mechanism for that deeper repentance was not fully available.

At Pentecost, something changed. Acts 2:38 — "Repent and be baptised, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins. And you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit." The repentance of Acts 2:38 is not primarily behavioural reformation. It is the turning of the whole person toward Jesus — acknowledging who he is and receiving what he has done. And the gift that follows — the Holy Spirit — is the one who produces from the inside the transformation that behavioural effort was never able to produce from the outside.

Ezekiel 36:26-27 — "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit in you; I will remove from you your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh. And I will put my Spirit in you and move you to follow my decrees and be careful to keep my laws." This is the New Covenant mechanism: not try harder, but receive the Spirit who produces obedience from within. Repentance is the door — the turning toward Christ — and the Holy Spirit is the life that flows through it.

Times of Refreshing

Acts 3:19 promises something that the guilt cycle never delivers: "Repent, then, and turn to God, so that your sins may be wiped out, that times of refreshing may come from the Lord."

Times of refreshing. Not: times of sustained effort. Not: periods of stricter self-discipline. Times of refreshing — the word suggests breath, coolness, renewal. The image is of someone who has been in a hot, airless place and walks into a breeze.

The guilt cycle produces exhaustion. Genuine repentance produces refreshing. This is one of the clearest diagnostic tests: if your repentance is leaving you more exhausted and more burdened over time, it may be worldly sorrow dressed as repentance. Genuine repentance — the turning toward Christ, the receiving of forgiveness, the receiving of the Spirit — produces the refreshing that Acts 3:19 describes.

Repentance Is Not a One-Time Event

One more clarification that the New Testament requires. Repentance is not only the initial turning that begins the Christian life. It is the ongoing posture of the Christian life.

Revelation 2-3 — the letters to the seven churches — contain the word "repent" five times, addressed to existing churches with existing believers. The call to repent is not only for unbelievers receiving the gospel for the first time. It is the regular, ongoing calling of the community that is in Christ to keep turning toward him — away from the drift, the compromise, the cold love, the tolerance of what should not be tolerated.

1 John 1:9 — "if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just and will forgive us our sins and purify us from all unrighteousness." This is written to believers. The regular confession and turning that John describes is not a crisis experience. It is the daily posture of the person who is walking with Christ — honest about failure, quick to turn, confident in the faithfulness of the one who forgives.

The goal is not a single dramatic turning followed by perfect consistency. The goal is a life increasingly oriented toward Christ — with the turnings becoming more natural, the drift shorter, the return quicker, as the Spirit works his transformation from the inside out.
