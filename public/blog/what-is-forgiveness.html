FORGIVENESS

What Is Forgiveness? Does God Really Forgive Everything?

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

There is a specific kind of person who searches this question. Not someone who is academically curious about the theology of atonement. Someone who has done something — or many things — that they have not been able to move past. Something that replays. Something that, when it surfaces in the quiet moments, produces a weight that no amount of time has fully lifted.

And the question underneath the question is not really "what is forgiveness?" It is this: "Does it extend to what I did?"

This article answers that question directly. Not with reassuring generalities. From the Scriptures — specifically, with the legal precision that the New Testament uses when it describes what forgiveness actually accomplishes and how far it actually reaches.

What Forgiveness Is Not

Before defining what forgiveness is, it helps to name two things it is not — because both of these counterfeits are common enough to cause confusion.

Forgiveness is not pretending the wrong did not happen. God does not forgive sin by overlooking it — by deciding that, on reflection, it was not as serious as it seemed. A judge who dismisses genuine crimes because he has decided they were not really wrong is not merciful. He is corrupt. The Old Testament is explicit about this: Proverbs 17:15 — "acquitting the guilty and condemning the innocent — the Lord detests them both." Divine forgiveness does not work by reclassifying sin as not-sin. The sin was real. Its weight was real. Its cost was real.

Forgiveness is not the same as forgetting. Jeremiah 31:34 says God "will remember their sins no more" — but this is not a statement about divine memory failure. It is a legal declaration: God will not hold those sins on record as a charge against the person. The event may still be known. The consequences may still have played out in the world. But the charge — the legal standing of the sin before God — has been cancelled. It will not be brought up again.

Understanding what forgiveness is not protects us from two errors: the error of thinking forgiveness means sin was not serious (it was), and the error of thinking we must somehow erase the past before forgiveness is fully received (we cannot, and we do not need to).

The Legal Image — The Debt Cancelled

The most precise image the New Testament uses for forgiveness is a legal and financial one. Colossians 2:13-14 — "When you were dead in your sins and in the uncircumcision of your flesh, God made you alive with Christ. He forgave us all our sins, having cancelled the charge of our legal indebtedness, which stood against us and condemned us; he has taken it away, nailing it to the cross."

Three images in rapid sequence. Each one matters.

The charge of legal indebtedness. In the ancient world, when a person incurred a debt they could not pay, a written record was kept — a certificate of debt, a cheirographon in Greek — listing what was owed. Paul uses this image for sin: our sins created a legal record, a certificate of indebtedness before God's moral law, that we had no means of paying off.

Cancelled. The Greek word (exaleipho) means to wipe out, to obliterate, to erase completely. Not: partially reduced. Not: held in suspension pending future good behaviour. Cancelled — the record wiped clean, the certificate no longer valid, the debt no longer standing.

Nailed to the cross. The image is specific and visual. When Roman authorities released a prisoner, they sometimes nailed the cancelled charge to the prison door — the public declaration that the debt had been paid and the person was free. Paul says the certificate of our sin-debt has been nailed to the cross of Christ — publicly, permanently, visibly declared cancelled. It is not stored somewhere, awaiting retrieval if we sin again. It has been nailed. It is done.

The Scope — All Your Sins

The most important word in Colossians 2:13 is the one that is easiest to read past: "He forgave us all our sins."

Not some. Not the smaller ones. Not the ones that fit within a reasonable scale of human failure. All. Every sin — past, present, and future — was present before God's foreknowledge when Christ went to the cross, and all of it was nailed to the cross with him.

1 John 1:9 states the same scope with different language: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just and will forgive us our sins and purify us from all unrighteousness." All unrighteousness. The word "all" in that sentence does not have an exception clause, a footnote, or a threshold of severity beyond which it no longer applies.

Hebrews 10:17 quotes Jeremiah 31:34 in the context of the New Covenant: "Their sins and lawless acts I will remember no more." And then verse 18 draws the conclusion: "And where these have been forgiven, sacrifice for sins is no longer necessary." Where forgiveness has been given — fully, completely, finally — there is nothing left to pay. The sacrifice was once for all. The debt was once for all. The forgiveness is once for all.

Isaiah 1:18 gives the image that has captured the scope of forgiveness for three thousand years: "Though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they are red as crimson, they shall be like wool." Scarlet — the most vivid, most permanent dye known in the ancient world, the one most impossible to remove. White as snow. Not: lighter. Not: less noticeable. White as snow. The most complete transformation of colour imaginable.

Is There Anything Beyond Forgiveness?

This is the real question. Not: does forgiveness exist? But: does it extend to what I specifically did?

The New Testament's answer is structured to remove every possible exception.

Romans 8:1 — "There is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." No condemnation — not: no condemnation for those whose sins were below a certain severity. No condemnation — for those who are in Christ Jesus. The single condition is union with Christ, not the nature or magnitude of the sin.

Romans 8:38-39 — nothing in all creation can separate the believer from the love of God in Christ Jesus. Nothing. The list that Paul gives is exhaustive and ends with the catch-all phrase "nor anything else in all creation." Your specific sin is part of creation. It is covered by the "anything else."

Matthew 12:31 is the one verse that appears to set a limit: "every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven, but blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven." This verse has caused enormous anguish to people who fear they have committed the unforgivable sin. But the context of Matthew 12 is specific: the religious leaders were watching Jesus cast out demons and attributing his work to Satan (Matthew 12:24). The unforgivable sin is the sustained, final, deliberate rejection of the Spirit's testimony about Jesus — the hardening of the heart to the point where no repentance is any longer sought or desired.

The diagnostic is simple and has been noted by many careful readers: the person who fears they have committed the unforgivable sin has almost certainly not committed it. The very fact of the fear — the concern, the desire to be forgiven, the reaching toward God — is evidence of the Spirit's ongoing work. The person who has finally and fully rejected the Spirit does not reach toward God. The reaching is itself evidence that the door is still open.

The Specific Guilt — The Sin You Cannot Forgive Yourself For

The pastoral reality that this article was written to address is not theological uncertainty about the scope of divine forgiveness. It is the experience of self-condemnation — the inability to receive for yourself what you intellectually know God has given.

2 Corinthians 7:10 — "Godly sorrow brings repentance that leads to salvation and leaves no regret." The phrase "leaves no regret" — the Greek is ametameletos, meaning without afterthought, without the lingering backward glance that replays and rehears the sin. Genuine repentance, fully received, does not leave the person perpetually rehearsing the sin. The rehearsal is a sign that the forgiveness has been known about but not yet received.

The inability to forgive oneself is often, at its root, a refusal to accept that the cross was sufficient. It is the implicit belief that the specific sin — this particular one, with all its weight and history — is the exception that Christ's blood does not cover. This belief, however sincere, is a contradiction of what the cross accomplished. To refuse to receive forgiveness is not humility. It is the denial of the sufficiency of what Christ did.

Hebrews 9:14 — "How much more, then, will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself unblemished to God, cleanse our consciences from acts that lead to death, so that we may serve the living God!" The blood of Christ cleanses the conscience — specifically, the conscience that is burdened by acts that lead to death. The cleansing is internal. The peace that follows genuine reception of forgiveness is not the peace of having repaired the past. It is the peace of knowing the past has been nailed to the cross and the charge no longer stands.

The Scarlet Cord — A Picture of Forgiveness

The devotional series He Entered Before He Came traces the scarlet cord from Rahab's window in Joshua 2 to the cross of Calvary. Rahab — a woman whose history was public and whose past was exactly the kind of history that religious culture would use to define her — hung a scarlet cord in her window and was protected when judgment fell. The colour of the cord was the colour of blood. The protection it provided was total — everyone inside the walls it marked was safe.

Matthew 1:5 places Rahab in the genealogy of Jesus. The woman with the scarlet cord in the window walks out of the ruins of Jericho and into the family tree of the Messiah — not despite her history, but with it, redeemed and repositioned by grace.

Isaiah 1:18 says sins that are scarlet become white as snow. The scarlet cord was always pointing toward the blood that makes the scarlet-sin white. And the people inside the walls of that forgiveness are not defined by what the cord covered. They are defined by the one who provided it.
