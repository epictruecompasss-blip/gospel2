Can a Christian Lose Their Salvation? The Full Biblical Answer

By Sir Henry M M  ·  In Him Daily

Can a Christian Lose Their Salvation? The Full Biblical Answer

This is one of the most visited theological questions on the internet — and the reason is not academic curiosity. The person asking it has a specific history. They walked away from faith for years. They sinned badly after their conversion. They spent a long season far from God. And now they are back — or trying to come back — carrying the fear that what they had has been forfeited.

This article takes that fear seriously. It presents the genuine biblical tension honestly — both the security texts and the warning texts — because intellectual honesty requires both. And it resolves the question not by taking sides in the centuries-old theological debate but by standing on what the New Covenant specifically says about the mechanism of salvation's security.

Note: Article 12 in this series (Can I Lose My Salvation?) addressed the same question from the personal pastoral angle. This article goes deeper into the theological framework — the two major positions, their strongest arguments, and the New Covenant resolution that holds both sets of texts with full seriousness.

The Two Serious Positions

The question of eternal security has produced two major theological traditions within orthodox Christianity. Both take Scripture seriously. Both have been held by serious, Bible-honouring theologians for centuries. Both deserve their strongest argument, not a caricature.

The Calvinist / Reformed position (Perseverance of the Saints) holds that those who are truly regenerated — genuinely born again, genuinely united to Christ — will persevere in faith to the end. Not because they are strong enough to sustain their own faith, but because God is faithful to complete what he has begun (Philippians 1:6). The warning texts are understood as serving one of two functions: either they are addressed to those who were never truly regenerated (and whose falling away reveals this), or they are the very means by which God preserves the elect in faith — the warnings are how God keeps his people from falling away.

The strongest arguments for this position: John 10:28-29 (no one can snatch them from the Son's hand or the Father's hand — the double guarantee), Romans 8:38-39 (nothing in all creation can separate us from the love of God in Christ), Ephesians 1:13-14 (the Holy Spirit as seal and deposit guaranteeing the inheritance), Philippians 1:6 (he who began a good work will carry it to completion), and Jude 24 (he is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you faultless).

The Arminian / Wesleyan position (Conditional Security) holds that genuine believers can, through sustained apostasy and the final rejection of Christ, lose the salvation they genuinely received. The warning texts are not hypothetical or addressed only to false converts — they are serious warnings to real believers about a real possibility. The grace of God does not override human freedom to the point of making apostasy impossible.

The strongest arguments for this position: Hebrews 6:4-6 (it is impossible to renew to repentance those who have once been enlightened, tasted the heavenly gift, shared in the Holy Spirit, and fallen away), Hebrews 10:26-29 (if we deliberately keep sinning after receiving knowledge of the truth, no sacrifice remains), 2 Peter 2:20-22 (those who have escaped the corruption of the world and are then again entangled are worse off than before), and the Galatians warnings about falling from grace (Galatians 5:4).

Both positions have things to teach us. Neither should be dismissed. And neither fully satisfies — because the tension in the biblical text is real and both traditions have spent centuries wrestling with the texts the other side emphasises.

What Neither Position Should Do

Before offering the New Covenant resolution, it is worth naming two errors that the debate about eternal security has historically produced.

Error 1 — Using security to license careless living. Romans 6:1-2 anticipated this error immediately after Paul's fullest treatment of grace in Romans 5: "Shall we go on sinning so that grace may increase? By no means! We are those who have died to sin; how can we live in it any longer?" The doctrine of eternal security, properly understood, produces not carelessness about sin but a deeper aversion to it — because the person who has genuinely received grace is a person who has been changed by it, not someone unchanged who is merely protected despite living unchanged.

Error 2 — Using the warning texts to produce perpetual terror. The warning texts exist as means of preservation, not instruments of torment. The person who reads Hebrews 6 and is sobered by it — who feels the weight of what falling away would mean and is moved to greater faithfulness — is precisely the person the warning is designed to help. The person who reads it and concludes that their salvation is always teetering on the edge of forfeiture, that every stumble might be the final fall, that God is perpetually one sin away from withdrawing what he gave — that person has misread the warning's purpose.

The New Covenant Resolution — Three Pillars

The New Covenant provides a specific framework for understanding the security of salvation that both traditions draw from but neither fully centres on. Three pillars hold the New Covenant security:

Pillar 1 — The Seal of the Spirit (Ephesians 4:30)

"Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, with whom you were sealed for the day of redemption." The Spirit is the seal — the mark of divine ownership and the guarantee of final delivery. A seal in the ancient world was placed by the owner on something that belonged to them, indicating: this is mine, this is under my protection, this will reach its destination.

The sealing is "for the day of redemption" — the seal is designed to last until the final consummation. It is not a provisional mark subject to removal at every serious sin. It is the Spirit's own presence in the believer as the guarantee of ultimate delivery.

Ephesians 1:13-14 develops the same image: "Having believed, you were marked in him with a seal, the promised Holy Spirit, who is a deposit guaranteeing our inheritance until the redemption of those who are God's possession." A deposit in the ancient commercial world was the down payment that legally committed the buyer to complete the transaction. The Spirit is God's own binding commitment to complete what the seal has marked. The inheritance will be delivered because the deposit has been given.

Pillar 2 — The Intercessory Work of Christ (Hebrews 7:25)

"He is able to save completely those who come to God through him, because he always lives to intercede for them."

This is the most decisive verse for understanding what sustains the believer's salvation. The mechanism is not the believer's sustained faithfulness. It is Christ's permanent, unceasing intercession. He always lives to intercede — the present tense is emphatic and continuous. Right now, the risen Christ is interceding for every believer. Not just in the seasons of spiritual health and consistent obedience. Always.

Luke 22:31-32 shows what this intercession looks like in practice. Jesus said to Peter — before Peter's denial — "Simon, Simon, Satan has asked to sift all of you as wheat. But I have prayed for you, Simon, that your faith may not fail. And when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers." Jesus knew Peter was about to deny him. He interceded before the failure, not after. And the intercession was specific: that your faith may not fail. Not: that you will not sin. That your faith — the root from which repentance and return grow — will not fail.

Peter's faith did not fail. He sinned badly, denied Christ three times, and wept bitterly. But he returned. His faith held because Christ had prayed for it. That is what Hebrews 7:25 means: the permanence of Christ's intercession is the mechanism that keeps the believer's faith from final failure.

Pillar 3 — The Once-For-All Sacrifice (Hebrews 10:10-14)

"And by that will, we have been made holy through the sacrifice of the body of Jesus Christ once for all... because by one sacrifice he has made perfect forever those who are being made holy."

The sacrifice that accomplished salvation was not provisional. It was once for all — the Greek ephapax, meaning once for all time, not to be repeated, not to be undone. The making holy was accomplished by a sacrifice that is complete and permanent. The "made perfect forever" is the New Covenant's strongest statement of the permanent accomplishment of the cross for those who are being sanctified.

Hebrews 10:17-18 quotes Jeremiah 31:34: "Their sins and lawless acts I will remember no more. And where these have been forgiven, sacrifice for sins is no longer necessary." The New Covenant forgiveness is not a running account that sin continues to deplete. It is a once-for-all cancellation of the record — with no further sacrifice needed because the one sufficient sacrifice is complete.

What About the Warning Texts?

The three pillars of New Covenant security do not make the warning texts disappear. They provide the context in which the warnings function as means of preservation rather than threats of forfeiture.

The warning of Hebrews 6:4-6 is addressed to people who have genuinely experienced the powers of the coming age and are in danger of finally and publicly repudiating Christ — not to Christians who are struggling and sinning and returning. The Hebrews community was under pressure to return to Judaism, to publicly deny that Jesus was the Messiah, to go back. That is the context of the warning. The sober reading of the warning by the struggling believer is itself evidence that they are not in the category the warning describes.

The person who walked away from faith for years and is now returning — whose question prompted this article — is not exhibiting the pattern of Hebrews 6. They are exhibiting the pattern of Luke 15 — the son who came back, the father who ran to meet them. The warning of Hebrews 6 is for those who have finally, publicly, permanently turned their backs on Christ. The returning prodigal has not done this. The very returning is proof of that.

The Word for the Person Who Is Coming Back

Jude 24-25 — "To him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you before his glorious presence without fault and with great joy — to the only God our Saviour be glory, majesty, power and authority, through Jesus Christ our Lord, before all ages, now and forevermore! Amen."

He is able to keep you from stumbling. The ability is his, not ours. He is able to present you faultless — not because you arrived faultless, but because the one who presents you is the one whose sacrifice covered every fault.

The believer who is returning from a long season of absence is not returning to a God who has been calculating how long they were away. They are returning to the Father who has been watching the road and who runs toward the returning figure before the speech is ready.
