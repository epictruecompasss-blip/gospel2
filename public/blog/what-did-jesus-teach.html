WHO IS JESUS

What Did Jesus Teach? His Most Important Words

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

Most summaries of Jesus' teaching reduce him to a moral philosopher — someone who said love your neighbour, treat others well, and live a good life. This misses almost everything. Jesus was not primarily a moral teacher. He was making the most audacious claim in human history — and his teaching was inseparable from that claim. To understand what Jesus taught, you have to understand who Jesus claimed to be.

This article traces the four great pillars of Jesus' teaching: the Kingdom of God, the Sermon on the Mount, the parables, and the I AM statements. Together they do not produce a moral code. They produce an encounter with a person.

The Kingdom of God — The Central Theme

If you had to summarise everything Jesus taught in two words, they would be these: Kingdom of God. Mark 1:15 records the very first words of Jesus' public ministry: "The time has come. The kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news."

The Kingdom of God is not a place. It is a reign — the dynamic, active rule of God breaking into human history in the person and work of Jesus Christ. Every parable Jesus told, every miracle he performed, every teaching he gave was a declaration that the Kingdom was arriving. Not in the future. Now. In him.

This is why Jesus' teaching is different from every other religious teacher's. Confucius taught a moral code. Buddha taught a path. Mohammed taught a law. Jesus did not primarily teach any of these things. He announced an event — the arrival of God's reign — and invited people to enter it.

Matthew 13 contains seven parables about the Kingdom, each revealing a different dimension of its nature. The Kingdom is like a mustard seed — it begins invisibly small and grows to fill everything. It is like yeast hidden in dough — it works invisibly until the whole is leavened. It is like treasure hidden in a field — worth selling everything to possess. It is like a merchant seeking fine pearls — the one who finds it gives up everything else for it.

In every parable the Kingdom has the same quality: it is worth everything. It is the one thing for which all other things are reasonable to sacrifice. And it arrives not through human achievement but through the action of God — hidden, quiet, unstoppable.

The Sermon on the Mount — The Character of the Kingdom

Matthew 5-7 contains the longest continuous block of Jesus' teaching in the Gospels. It is commonly called the Sermon on the Mount, and it is almost universally misread.

The most common misreading is to treat it as a new and harder moral law — Jesus raising the bar from the Old Testament's external requirements to internal ones. Do not commit adultery is not enough; do not even look with lust. Do not murder is not enough; do not even be angry with your brother. The logic of this reading is that the Sermon on the Mount is Jesus telling us to try harder.

But this misses the opening verses — the Beatitudes — which establish the framework for everything that follows. Blessed are the poor in spirit. Blessed are those who mourn. Blessed are the meek. These are not achievements. They are states of neediness. Jesus does not open the Sermon by saying blessed are those who have worked hard enough. He opens it by blessing the people who know they are not enough.

The Sermon on the Mount is not a new law to perform. It is a description of Kingdom life — the life that becomes possible when the Kingdom has arrived and the King himself is present. Matthew 5:17 — "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfil them." He fulfils the law — which means the law's demands are met in him and through him, not by us achieving them independently.

The commands of the Sermon on the Mount are not the entrance requirements for the Kingdom. They are the shape of the life that flows from being in the Kingdom — from knowing the King.

The Lord's Prayer — The Shape of Kingdom Living

Embedded in the Sermon on the Mount is the prayer that Jesus gave his disciples when they asked him how to pray (Matthew 6:9-13). It is not primarily a formula to recite. It is a framework that reveals what the Kingdom life looks like in practice.

Our Father in heaven — the first word establishes the relationship. Not a distant deity to be appeased. A Father. Accessible. Personal. Near.

Hallowed be your name — the first request is not for personal need but for divine honour. Before we bring our requests, we orient ourselves around who God is.

Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven — the primary petition is not for personal comfort but for divine rule. We are asking for the Kingdom to arrive fully in every corner of the world as it has arrived in us.

Give us today our daily bread — only here does personal need appear. And it is deliberately small and daily. Not annual. Not lifetime. Today. The prayer trains us to depend on God one day at a time.

Forgive us our debts as we also have forgiven our debtors — forgiveness received and forgiveness given are inseparable in Kingdom living. The person who has truly received forgiveness will find it possible to extend it. The person who cannot extend it has not yet fully received it.

Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil — the acknowledgment that we need ongoing protection. The Kingdom has arrived but the battle is not yet over. We need daily deliverance.

The Parables — Truth Told Sideways

Jesus told approximately forty parables — short stories drawn from everyday life that carry the weight of the Kingdom's deepest truths. He explained to his disciples why he taught this way: "The knowledge of the secrets of the kingdom of heaven has been given to you, but not to them" (Matthew 13:11).

The parables are not simply illustrations that make abstract truth easier to understand. They are revelatory — they open truth to those who are genuinely seeking while concealing it from those who are only intellectually curious. The same story lands differently depending on where the hearer is.

Three parables from Luke 15 form the theological heart of Jesus' teaching about God's character. A shepherd leaves ninety-nine sheep to find one lost one. A woman sweeps her entire house to find one lost coin. A father runs to meet his returning son while he is still far off. Each parable says the same thing with increasing emotional intensity: God seeks the lost. God celebrates the found. And his seeking is not passive waiting — it is active, urgent, self-giving pursuit.

Luke 15:20 contains one of the most theologically loaded sentences Jesus ever spoke: "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son, threw his arms around him and kissed him." The father ran. In the first-century Near Eastern world, a dignified older man did not run. Running required gathering up your robes, exposing your legs, abandoning the posture of authority. The father in this parable abandons dignity for the sake of the returning son.

This is not a teaching about how people should behave toward the wayward. It is a revelation of who God is.

The I AM Statements — When Teaching Becomes Claim

The most distinctive feature of Jesus' teaching — the element that makes it unlike any other religious teaching in history — is the way it constantly circles back to himself. He does not point away from himself toward a teaching. He points to himself as the teaching.

The seven I AM statements of John's Gospel each begin with the divine name — "I AM" — that God gave Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14). Jesus is not using a grammatical construction. He is making a claim to divine identity and then specifying what that identity means for the person standing in front of him.

"I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never go hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty" (John 6:35). Jesus is not teaching about spiritual nourishment. He is claiming to be the source of it.

"I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will never walk in darkness, but will have the light of life" (John 8:12). He is not teaching about enlightenment. He is claiming to be the light.

"I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6). This is the most exclusive claim in the history of religion. He is not claiming to have found the way. He is claiming to be it.

C.S. Lewis made the argument that cannot be avoided: a person who said these things is either a liar, a lunatic, or Lord. A great moral teacher who said these things would not be a great moral teacher — he would be making the most deluded claims any human being has ever made. The only coherent reading is that he meant exactly what he said, and that what he said is true.

The Teaching That Points to the Teacher

What Did Jesus teach? He taught about the Kingdom of God that was arriving in him. He taught about the character of the Kingdom life — the Sermon on the Mount. He told parables that revealed the heart of the Father who runs toward the returning child. And he made the I AM statements that made his teaching inseparable from his identity.

The cumulative effect of all of it is not a moral code but an invitation. Matthew 11:28-29: "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls."

The invitation of Jesus is not to a programme but to a person. Not to a set of teachings to master but to a relationship with the Teacher. And the rest he promises is not the rest of having successfully completed the moral requirements. It is the rest of the person who has stopped trying to achieve what can only be received.
