How Do I Pray? A Practical Biblical Guide

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

Most people who ask "how do I pray?" are not asking for a theology of prayer. They are carrying something more personal than that. The sense that they are doing it wrong. The fear that God is not actually hearing. The disappointment of praying for something important and receiving what felt like silence. The awkwardness of not knowing what words to use or how long to go or whether what they feel — or do not feel — during prayer means anything.

This article addresses all of those concerns. But it begins with the most important correction first: prayer is not a technique to master. It is a relationship to inhabit.

The Misconception That Makes Prayer Hard

The most common reason prayer feels difficult is that people approach it as a performance — a spiritual activity that must be executed correctly to produce the desired result. Under this view, the right words, the right posture, the right emotional intensity, and the right level of faith are all variables that determine whether the prayer "works."

This view of prayer is not the biblical one. And it produces exactly the experience that drives most people's frustration with prayer: constant uncertainty about whether they did it right, accompanied by the nagging suspicion that if the answer does not come, the fault lies in some inadequacy of their praying.

Jesus addressed this misconception directly in Matthew 6:7-8 — "And when you pray, do not keep on babbling like pagans, for they think they will be heard because of their many words. Do not be like them, for your Father knows what you need before you ask him."

Your Father knows what you need before you ask him. This single sentence dismantles the performance view of prayer entirely. Prayer is not the mechanism by which you inform God of your needs. He already knows. Prayer is not the means by which you generate enough spiritual pressure to move an indifferent deity to act. He already cares. Prayer is the activity of a child talking to a Father who was already paying attention before the conversation began.

The Lord's Prayer — A Framework, Not a Formula

Matthew 6:9-13 gives the prayer Jesus taught his disciples — commonly called the Lord's Prayer. The introduction is important: "This, then, is how you should pray" — not "these are the exact words to say." Jesus gave a framework for the shape of prayer, not a formula to be recited.

Six movements. Each one reveals something about what prayer is and what it is for.

"Our Father in heaven" — the address establishes the relationship before any request is made. Not "O great and distant deity." Not "supreme being." Father. The word Jesus used — Abba — is the intimate, familial address of a child to a parent. Prayer begins by locating yourself in a relationship, not launching a petition.

The word "our" matters equally. Prayer is never entirely private. Even when you pray alone, you pray as part of a community — the "our" connects you to every other person who is praying the same prayer in the same relationship with the same Father.

"Hallowed be your name" — the first petition is not personal need. It is divine honour. Before we bring our requests, we orient ourselves around who God is. The name of God in the Hebrew tradition is not merely a label but a representation of character — all that God is. To hallow his name is to pray that his character would be known, honoured, and reflected in the world.

This ordering is not accidental. Beginning prayer with God's honour rather than our need is the posture that keeps prayer from becoming a wish list presented to a vending machine.

"Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" — the primary petition is not for personal comfort but for divine rule. We are asking for the reign of God to arrive fully — in our lives, our families, our communities, our world — as completely as it exists in the heavenly realm where God's will is done without resistance or delay.

This petition also addresses the deepest fear that surrounds unanswered prayer: if God's will is different from what I am asking for, is God failing me? No — we are invited to pray that his will be done. The submission to divine will is not resignation. It is trust that the one who knows everything and loves completely is directing all things toward a good that our limited perspective cannot fully see.

"Give us today our daily bread" — only here does personal need appear. And it is deliberately small and daily. Not annual provision. Not lifetime security. Today. The prayer that Jesus gave trains us to depend on God in the daily rhythm — not to secure enough provision to make ongoing dependence unnecessary, but to return each day to the one who gives.

This is the prayer that the wilderness generation received in the form of manna — enough for today, none left over for tomorrow, impossible to hoard. The daily bread petition keeps the pray-er in the posture of the child who needs the Father daily rather than the adult who has accumulated enough to manage independently.

"Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" — forgiveness received and forgiveness extended are presented as inseparable. This is not a transactional formula — as if God's forgiveness is conditional on our forgiving others. It is a description of the reality: the person who has genuinely received forgiveness will find it increasingly possible to extend it. The person who is unable to forgive others has likely not yet fully received what was freely given to them.

"Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil" — the final petition is the acknowledgment that we need ongoing protection. The Kingdom has arrived but the battle is not over. The believer who prays this prayer daily is the believer who is not overconfident about their own strength. The request for deliverance is not weakness. It is wisdom.

Does God Actually Hear?

The fear that God does not actually hear — that prayer disappears into a void — is addressed throughout the New Testament with a directness that leaves no room for uncertainty.

Matthew 7:7-8 — "Ask and it will be given to you; seek and you will find; knock and the door will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives; the one who seeks finds; and to the one who knocks, the door will be opened." The universal scope of these verbs — everyone who asks, the one who seeks — is not a promise that every prayer is answered exactly as asked. It is the promise that prayer is not ignored. The ask, seek, knock progression suggests persistence and movement — the imagery of someone who keeps returning to the door rather than asking once and walking away.

1 John 5:14-15 — "This is the confidence we have in approaching God: that if we ask anything according to his will, he hears us. And if we know that he hears us — whatever we ask — we know that we have what we asked of him." He hears. The confidence is not that every request is granted on our terms. It is that none of them disappear unheard.

Hebrews 4:16 — "Let us then approach God's throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need." The throne of grace approached with confidence — not tentatively, not with the uncertainty of someone unsure whether they will be received. Confidence. Because the one on the throne is a Father who was already paying attention before you arrived.

Am I Supposed to Feel Something?

The absence of feeling during prayer is one of the most common sources of doubt about whether prayer is "working." If I do not feel anything — no warmth, no peace, no sense of divine presence — does that mean God was not there?

The New Testament does not make feeling the test of genuine prayer. Romans 8:26-27 — "We do not know what we ought to pray for, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us through wordless groans. And he who searches our hearts knows the mind of the Spirit, because the Spirit intercedes for God's people in accordance with the will of God."

The Spirit intercedes for us through what we cannot articulate — wordless groans, inarticulate longings, the prayers that cannot be formed into words. This means that the prayer that feels most inadequate — the prayer offered in exhaustion or grief or confusion, when no words come — is not the prayer least heard. It may be the prayer most attended to, because the Spirit is translating what cannot be expressed and carrying it directly to the Father.

Feeling something during prayer is a gift when it comes. It is not the evidence of answered prayer when it does not. The reality of prayer's reception rests on the character of the one receiving it, not the emotional intensity of the one offering it.

A Simple Place to Begin

For the person who does not know where to start: start with the Lord's Prayer itself. Not as a ritual to be repeated mechanically but as a framework to inhabit. Take each petition and expand it — make it personal, specific, honest. What does "hallowed be your name" mean in your specific situation today? What is "your daily bread" for the week you are in? What needs forgiving, and who do you need to forgive?

Then add what is specific to you. The names of the people you are carrying. The situation that is keeping you awake. The thing you need and do not have. The gratitude for what has arrived. Prayer is a conversation with a Father who already knows what you need — but who, like every good father, wants to hear it from you anyway.
