THE TRINITY

What Is the Trinity? God in Three Persons Explained

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

The Trinity is the most misunderstood doctrine in Christianity — and also the most important. It is misunderstood by critics who call it illogical. It is misunderstood by believers who find it confusing. It is sometimes badly explained as a puzzle to be solved rather than a person to be known.

This article does not attempt to fully explain the Trinity — because the Trinity, by its nature, exceeds what human categories can contain. What this article does is trace what the Bible actually says, address the most common objections and misunderstandings, and show why the Trinity is not a philosophical burden on the Christian faith but the most beautiful revelation at its centre.

The Starting Point — What the Trinity Is Not

Before describing what the Trinity is, it helps to name what it is not — because the most common alternatives are actually ancient heresies that distort the biblical picture.

It is not three gods (tritheism). Christianity is not polytheism with three deities who happen to work together. The Old and New Testaments are both unambiguous: there is one God. Deuteronomy 6:4 — "Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one." 1 Corinthians 8:4 — "there is no God but one." The Trinity does not compromise monotheism.

It is not one God in three masks (modalism). Some attempt to explain the Trinity by saying that God appears as Father in the Old Testament, as Son during Jesus' ministry, and as Spirit since Pentecost — one person cycling through three modes. This is called modalism and it is explicitly ruled out by passages where all three persons appear simultaneously. At Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:16-17), the Son is baptised, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father speaks from heaven. Three persons, one moment, distinguished from each other.

It is not a hierarchy where only the Father is truly God. Jehovah's Witnesses and others teach that Jesus is a created being — the first and greatest of God's creations, but not God himself. This is called Arianism (after the fourth-century teacher Arius who proposed it) and it was definitively rejected at the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD — not because the council invented Christ's divinity but because it had always been the church's teaching, under attack for the first time from Arius.

What the Trinity Is

The doctrine of the Trinity holds three things simultaneously:

There is only one God.

The Father is God. The Son is God. The Holy Spirit is God.

The Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are distinct from each other — not three names for the same person.

All three of these statements are taught in the New Testament. None of them can be abandoned without distorting the biblical picture. The theological term that holds them together is this: one God in three persons, co-equal and co-eternal.

This is not illogical. It would be illogical to say that God is one person and three persons simultaneously, or one God and three gods simultaneously. The Trinity says neither of these things. It says one God, three persons — and that distinction between "God" (one) and "persons" (three) is the key that makes the doctrine coherent.

The Biblical Evidence — All Three Persons

The New Testament did not invent the Trinity as a later theological development. The triune nature of God is present in the earliest Christian documents and is embedded in the narrative of Jesus' ministry.

The Father is the most natural referent when the New Testament speaks of "God." Jesus addresses the Father in prayer (John 17), speaks of the Father sending him (John 3:16), and distinguishes himself from the Father (John 14:28 — "the Father is greater than I").

The Son — Jesus Christ is identified as God throughout the New Testament. John 1:1 — "the Word was God." John 20:28 — Thomas addresses the risen Christ as "my Lord and my God." Colossians 2:9 — "in Christ all the fullness of the Deity lives in bodily form." Hebrews 1:8 — the Father addresses the Son as "God." These are not marginal texts. They are the mainstream of New Testament christology.

The Holy Spirit is identified as God in Acts 5:3-4 — when Peter says that Ananias has lied to the Holy Spirit, he immediately equates this with lying to God. The Spirit has the attributes of God: omniscience (1 Corinthians 2:10-11), omnipresence (Psalm 139:7-8), and is the agent of creation (Genesis 1:2).

All three together — The most decisive texts are those where all three persons appear together. Matthew 28:19 — "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." Note: not names (plural) but name (singular) — one name that encompasses three persons. 2 Corinthians 13:14 — "the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all." The three persons are distinguished but united in a single benediction.

Why the Trinity Is Not a Later Invention

A common objection is that the Trinity was invented at the Council of Nicaea in AD 325 — that the early church believed Jesus was merely a great man, and that the Council imposed divinity on him for political reasons.

This objection does not survive contact with the actual historical evidence.

The Council of Nicaea was called specifically to address the teaching of Arius — who was saying that Jesus was a created being, the first and greatest of God's creatures, but not God himself. The Council rejected this teaching. The significant historical point is this: you only call a council to address a teaching that contradicts what was previously held. Nicaea was defending the long-held belief in Christ's divinity against Arius's innovation — not inventing a new belief.

Paul's letters, written in the AD 50s — within twenty years of Jesus' death — already contain fully developed trinitarian thinking. Galatians 4:4-6 — "God sent his Son... that we might receive adoption to sonship. Because you are his sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts." Father, Son, Spirit — all three persons, all three distinct, all three active — in a letter written decades before Nicaea.

The Didache, a Christian document from approximately AD 100, contains the same trinitarian baptismal formula as Matthew 28:19. The early church fathers — Ignatius of Antioch (died around AD 110), Justin Martyr (died around AD 165), Irenaeus (died around AD 202) — all wrote about the Trinity long before Nicaea.

Nicaea did not invent the Trinity. It named it, defined the language for it, and defended it against a specific attack. The belief had always been there.

The Trinity and the Cross

The Trinity is not an abstract doctrine. It is the inner logic of the gospel.

The cross makes theological sense only within a trinitarian framework. If Jesus were merely a human being — even the greatest human being who ever lived — his death could atone for, at most, one other human life. The infinite weight of the accumulated sin of all humanity across all generations requires an atoning sacrifice of infinite worth. Only the death of the eternal Son of God — the one through whom all things were made (John 1:3) — has that worth.

Romans 8:3 — "God sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh to be a sin offering." The Father sent the Son. The Son came. The Spirit raised him from the dead (Romans 8:11). All three persons of the Trinity were active in the atonement.

The resurrection is equally trinitarian. Jesus said he would raise himself (John 10:17-18). Romans 8:11 says the Spirit raised him. Acts 2:32 says God raised him. Father, Son, and Spirit — all three active in the greatest event in human history.

The Trinity as the God You Were Made to Know

Here is the most important thing about the Trinity — the thing that gets lost when it is treated as a philosophical puzzle: the triune God is a community of love before he is anything else.

John 17:24 — Jesus prays to the Father: "you loved me before the creation of the world." Before anything was made, love existed — the love of the Father for the Son and the Son for the Father, in the fellowship of the Spirit. God is not solitary. He is relational. He is, in his own eternal being, a community.

And the invitation of the gospel is an invitation into that community. John 17:21 — "that all of them may be one, Father, just as you are in me and I am in you. May they also be in us." The prayer of Jesus is that his people would be drawn into the trinitarian relationship — not as fourth members of the Godhead, but as recipients of the love that the three persons have for each other.

1 John 4:8 — "God is love." Not just that God loves, but that love is what God is — in his own internal triune life, before anything outside himself existed. The Father loves the Son. The Son loves the Father. The Spirit is the bond and expression of that love. And all of creation, all of redemption, all of the gospel is the overflow of that internal divine love reaching outward to embrace what was made.

This is not a puzzle to solve. It is a person to know. Or rather — three persons, in one God, who opened the door and said: come in.
