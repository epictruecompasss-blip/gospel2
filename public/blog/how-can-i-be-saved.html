SALVATION

How Can I Be Saved? What the Bible Actually Says

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

This is the most urgent question a human being can ask. Not the most fashionable. Not the most comfortable. The most urgent. Because if the Bible is right about the human condition — if sin has genuinely separated us from the God who made us, and if death is not the end — then the question of how to be saved is more pressing than any other question life presents.

This article answers it directly, from the Scriptures, without manipulation and without conditions that the Bible itself does not place there. The answer is simpler than religion has often made it, and more demanding than culture has allowed it to be.

The Question Behind the Question

When people search for "how can I be saved," they are usually carrying one of three specific fears underneath the general question.

The first fear is that there is something they must do — some ritual, some formula, some level of sincerity they must achieve before salvation becomes available. They are afraid they have not done it correctly, or done enough of it, or felt the right things while doing it.

The second fear is that they have done too much wrong. That their particular history — the things they have said, the things they have done, the person they have been — places them outside the reach of whatever salvation is.

The third fear is that it is too late. That there was a window and it has closed, and whatever grace was available has been forfeited by too many years of walking in the wrong direction.

The biblical answer addresses all three fears directly. But it begins not with the fears but with the event that makes the answer possible: the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

The Day the Answer Was First Given

Acts 2 records the first time the question of salvation was answered after the resurrection of Jesus. Peter had just preached the first apostolic sermon — announcing that the Jesus whom the crowd had crucified had been raised from the dead and was now Lord and Messiah. The response of the crowd was the response of people who had just understood what they had done: "Brothers, what shall we do?" (Acts 2:37).

The question in Acts 2:37 is almost identical to the question this article is answering. What shall we do? How can we be saved? And Peter's answer in Acts 2:38 is the clearest, most direct answer to that question in the entire New Testament:

"Repent and be baptised, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins. And you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit."

Four elements. Every one of them matters. Not one of them is a performance to earn salvation. Together they describe what it looks like for a human being to receive what God has freely given.

Repent — What It Actually Means

Repentance is the most misunderstood word in the answer. It has been reduced to emotional remorse — feeling sufficiently bad about what you have done. Under this misreading, people spend years in cycles of guilt, confession, temporary relief, and more guilt — never quite sure if they have felt sorry enough to qualify.

The Greek word behind "repent" is metanoia — a compound word meaning to change (meta) your mind (noia). It is not primarily an emotional state. It is a directional change. A turning.

And crucially — in the context of Acts 2:38 — it is a turning TOWARD something, not merely away from something. The crowd has just heard that Jesus is Lord and Messiah. Repentance is the turning of the whole person toward that Jesus — acknowledging who he is, receiving what he has done, reorienting the direction of life around his lordship.

This is why repentance is not a performance of sufficient remorse. It is not about achieving a certain emotional intensity before God will accept you. It is about turning — genuinely, personally, with whatever you have — toward the Jesus who is already standing with his arms open.

Luke 15:20 shows what the Father does when someone turns: "while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son." You do not need to complete the journey before grace arrives. The turning itself — the first step in the right direction — is met by a Father who has already been watching and who runs toward you.

Be Baptised — The Public Declaration

Baptism in the New Testament is not a secondary step for the spiritually mature. It is the immediate, public response to the gospel — the outward declaration of the inward turning.

Romans 6:3-4 — "all of us who were baptised into Christ Jesus were baptised into his death... We were therefore buried with him through baptism into death in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may live a new life."

Baptism is the moment of public identification with the crucified and risen Christ. His death becomes your death. His resurrection becomes your life. It is not the baptism that saves — it is Christ who saves. But baptism is the declaration that you have turned, that you belong to him, and that the old life has been buried and the new life has begun.

In the New Testament, the gap between believing and being baptised is measured in hours, not months. The Ethiopian eunuch in Acts 8: he heard the gospel in a chariot, saw a body of water, and said "look, here is water — what can stand in the way of my being baptised?" (Acts 8:36). The same day. The same hour. The urgency of the response matched the weight of what had been received.

Receive Forgiveness — The Gift Already Given

"For the forgiveness of your sins." These words follow the call to repent and be baptised — not as a reward for having repented correctly, but as the content of what is received when a person turns to Christ.

Forgiveness is not offered after you have proven you are truly sorry. It is not conditional on achieving a certain standard of remorse. It is the free gift of God — already paid for at the cross, already available — received by the person who turns to Christ and is identified with him in baptism.

Colossians 2:13-14 — "When you were dead in your sins... God made you alive with Christ. He forgave us all our sins, having cancelled the charge of our legal indebtedness, which stood against us and condemned us; he has taken it away, nailing it to the cross."

All your sins. Not some. Not the smaller ones. Not the ones you confessed in the right way. All of them — cancelled, nailed to the cross, taken away. This is the scope of the forgiveness that is received when a person turns to Christ.

The fear that you have done too much wrong to be forgiven is specifically addressed by 1 John 1:9 — "if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just and will forgive us our sins and purify us from all unrighteousness." All unrighteousness. The word "all" in that verse does not have an exception clause.

Receive the Gift of the Holy Spirit — The New Life Begins

The fourth element of Acts 2:38 is the one most often treated as optional or secondary: "you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit."

It is not optional. It is not secondary. It is the new life that makes everything else possible. The Holy Spirit is not the reward for having been sufficiently spiritual. He is the gift — freely given to every person who turns to Christ. He is the indwelling presence of God in the believer — the one who produces the new character, enables the new life, seals the believer's eternal inheritance, and intercedes when words fail.

The word "gift" in Acts 2:38 is the same word used throughout the New Testament for grace — freely given, not earned, not merited, not purchased. You do not pray the Holy Spirit into your life through intensity. You do not receive the Spirit through giving money. You receive him the same way you receive forgiveness — by turning to Christ and trusting what has been freely given.

Is It Too Late?

Acts 2:39 immediately follows the salvation answer with the scope of the invitation: "The promise is for you and your children and for all who are far off — for all whom the Lord our God will call."

For all who are far off. The distance a person has travelled from God in years of walking in the wrong direction is not an obstacle to this promise. It is specifically named as the condition of those to whom the promise is extended.

There is one person in the New Testament whose story definitively answers the "is it too late" question. The thief on the cross (Luke 23:40-43) — crucified beside Jesus, with hours to live, a lifetime of crime behind him, no opportunity for baptism, no time for the moral reformation that might make the request seem more reasonable — said simply: "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom." And Jesus answered: "Truly I tell you, today you will be with me in paradise."

Today. Not eventually. Not if you survive and manage to do the right things. Today.

It is not too late. It was not too late for the thief on the cross with hours to live. It is not too late for you.

The Answer — Simply Stated

How can you be saved? Turn to Jesus Christ — acknowledging who he is and what he has done. Identify publicly with him in baptism. Receive the forgiveness that his death purchased. Receive the Holy Spirit who is the gift of new life.

Not by achieving sufficient remorse. Not by being good enough first. Not by waiting until you feel ready. By turning — now, with whatever you have, wherever you are — toward the one who is already running toward you.

Matthew 11:28 — "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest." The invitation is open. The door is not closed. The Father is running.
