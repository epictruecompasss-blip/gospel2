What Is the Kingdom of God? Jesus' Central Teaching Explained

By Sir Henry M M  ·  In Him Daily

What Is the Kingdom of God? Jesus' Central Teaching Explained

Jesus used the phrase "Kingdom of God" or "Kingdom of heaven" over one hundred times in the Gospels. It was not one topic among many for him. It was the central category of everything he taught. And yet most Christians could not define it clearly if asked — not because they have not read the Gospels, but because the phrase has been absorbed into Christian vocabulary without being fully understood.

This article traces what Jesus actually meant by the Kingdom of God — from its Old Testament roots through Jesus' own teaching and parables through the apostolic proclamation in Acts and into the full "already and not yet" framework that makes sense of everything the New Testament says about it.

The Word — Reign, Not Realm

The Greek phrase translated "Kingdom of God" is basileia tou theou. The word basileia is crucial: it does not primarily mean a place or territory. It means a reign — the dynamic, active, sovereign exercise of kingly authority. The Kingdom of God is not primarily a location you go to. It is the ruling activity of God breaking into and transforming reality.

This distinction changes everything. If the Kingdom of God is a place, then it is entirely future — something you enter after death. If the Kingdom of God is the reign of God — his active, sovereign governance — then it can be present wherever God's reign is being exercised, and it can break into history at a specific moment in history.

Which is exactly what Jesus announced.

The Kingdom in the Old Testament — The Coming Reign

The concept of God as King is present throughout the Old Testament. Psalm 93:1 — "The Lord reigns, he is robed in majesty." Psalm 103:19 — "The Lord has established his throne in heaven, and his kingdom rules over all." In one sense, the Kingdom of God has always existed — God has always been sovereign over all creation.

But the Old Testament also looks forward to a future, decisive, visible manifestation of that reign on earth — a moment when God's rule, which is currently contested by human rebellion and the powers of darkness, would be established in a way that could not be resisted or reversed.

Daniel 2:44 — "In the time of those kings, the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that will never be destroyed, nor will it be left to another people. It will crush all those other kingdoms and bring them to an end, but it will itself endure forever." Daniel 7:13-14 — "In my vision at night I looked, and there before me was one like a son of man, coming with the clouds of heaven... He was given authority, glory and sovereign power; all nations and peoples of every language worshipped him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion that will not pass away."

The coming of the Kingdom in the Old Testament prophetic vision is the coming of the Son of Man's dominion — everlasting, universal, final. And Jesus identified himself as that Son of Man.

The Kingdom Is Here — Jesus' Central Announcement

Mark 1:14-15 records the very first words of Jesus' public ministry: "The time has come. The kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news." The Kingdom of God has come near — not will come eventually, not is approaching as a distant future reality. Has come near. Is here. Now. In him.

This is the most important thing to understand about the Kingdom of God: Jesus did not merely teach about it. He embodied it. The Kingdom came near because the King came. Where Jesus was present, the Kingdom was present — in his teachings, his healings, his exorcisms, his table fellowship with sinners, his authority over nature.

Luke 11:20 — "But if I drive out demons by the finger of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you." The exorcisms were not merely miraculous displays. They were the Kingdom of God arriving in direct conflict with the kingdom of darkness. Every demon cast out was a declaration: the reign of God is here, and the powers that held this person are being displaced.

Matthew 12:28 — "But if it is by the Spirit of God that I drive out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you." Come upon you — the same word Paul uses in 1 Thessalonians 2:16 for something that has already arrived. The Kingdom of God was not merely imminent in Jesus' ministry. It was arriving, in him, through him, before the eyes of the people who watched him.

The Kingdom Parables — What the Arriving Kingdom Is Like

Matthew 13 contains seven parables of the Kingdom — each revealing a different dimension of its character. Together they describe a Kingdom that arrives not in overwhelming visible power but in ways that are initially hidden, small, and seemingly insignificant — yet ultimately transformative and unstoppable.

The mustard seed (Matthew 13:31-32) — the Kingdom begins as the smallest of seeds and grows to be the largest of garden plants, sheltering the birds of the air. The arrival is hidden. The growth is organic and unstoppable. The final extent is beyond what the beginning suggests.

The yeast (Matthew 13:33) — a small amount of yeast hidden in a large amount of dough. The Kingdom works invisibly, permeating everything it contacts, until the whole is leavened. The transformation is internal before it is external. The effect is larger than the cause appears.

The treasure hidden in a field (Matthew 13:44) — a man finds it and sells everything he has to buy the field. The Kingdom is worth more than everything else combined. The comparison is not between good things and the Kingdom. It is between everything and the Kingdom. Everything else is sold for the sake of it.

The merchant seeking fine pearls (Matthew 13:45-46) — the same principle from a different angle. The one pearl of great price is worth the sacrifice of every other pearl. No other treasure can compete.

The Kingdom Jesus describes in these parables is not a programme. It is not a political agenda. It is not a cultural project. It is the reign of God arriving in the world — hidden at first, growing unstoppably, worth everything to those who discover it.

The Already and the Not Yet

The most important framework for understanding the Kingdom of God is the "already and not yet" — a tension that runs through the entire New Testament.

The Kingdom has already come. Jesus announced its arrival. He embodied its presence. He defeated the powers at the cross (Colossians 2:15). He rose from the dead as the firstfruits of the new creation. He poured out the Spirit at Pentecost as the deposit of the Kingdom's full arrival. The Kingdom is already here — in the community of believers, in the presence of the Spirit, in the forgiveness and new life available through the gospel. Colossians 1:13 — "he has rescued us from the dominion of darkness and brought us into the kingdom of the Son he loves."

The Kingdom has not yet come in its fullness. The world is not yet fully under the visible, unopposed reign of God. Suffering continues. The powers have been defeated but not yet finally destroyed. Death has been conquered but not yet eliminated. The Kingdom in its fullness awaits the return of Christ — when every enemy is finally placed under his feet (1 Corinthians 15:25-26), when the kingdoms of this world become the Kingdom of our Lord (Revelation 11:15), when God dwells with his people and makes all things new (Revelation 21:3-5).

The believer lives in the overlap — between the inauguration of the Kingdom at the first coming of Christ and the consummation of the Kingdom at his return. Already forgiven, already receiving the Spirit, already citizens of the Kingdom — and not yet experiencing the Kingdom in its fullness. Already healed in the deepest sense — and not yet free from the physical limitations of the present age.

This "already and not yet" is the key to praying "your kingdom come" with integrity. We are not asking for something that has not yet begun. We are asking for what has begun to come in its fullness — the reign of God that is already here to arrive completely.

The Kingdom in Acts — What the Apostles Preached

The book of Acts confirms that the Kingdom of God was not merely Jesus' topic during his earthly ministry. It was the central category of apostolic preaching throughout the expansion of the early church.

Acts 8:12 — Philip in Samaria "proclaimed the good news of the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ." The Kingdom and the name of Jesus are paired — because Jesus is the King, and proclaiming the Kingdom means proclaiming the one who embodies it.

Acts 19:8 — Paul in Ephesus "spoke boldly there for three months, arguing persuasively about the kingdom of God." Acts 20:25 — Paul describes his ministry as "preaching the kingdom." Acts 28:23, 31 — in Rome, Paul "explained and declared to them the kingdom of God... he proclaimed the kingdom of God and taught about the Lord Jesus Christ."

The last word of the book of Acts — the summary of what Paul was doing while under house arrest in Rome — is that he was "proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance." The Kingdom of God is where the entire narrative of Acts lands. It is the frame within which the entire gospel makes sense.

The Land as Type — Joshua and the Kingdom

The devotional series He Entered Before He Came traces the promised land as one of the fullest Old Testament types of the Kingdom of God. The land was given before it was conquered. It belonged to Israel by divine promise before a single battle was fought. And the entering and occupying of the land was the living-out of what already belonged to them.

The Kingdom of God in the New Testament works the same way. It is given before it is fully experienced. Colossians 1:13 says believers have already been brought into the Kingdom of the Son. The Kingdom belongs to them by divine act, through the death and resurrection of the King. And the living out of the Kingdom life — faith, obedience, love, witness — is the occupying of what has already been given.

Ephesians 1:3 — "every spiritual blessing in the heavenly realms" is already given. Hebrews 4:3 — "we who have believed enter that rest." Present tense. The Kingdom is entered by faith, lived in by the Spirit, and will be consummated at the return of the King.

The Practical Implication — Seek First

Matthew 6:33 — "But seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well."

The practical implication of understanding the Kingdom of God is the reordering of everything else around it. The Kingdom is not a compartment of life — the religious hour on Sunday. It is the frame within which all of life is lived. Work, money, relationships, time, creativity, suffering, joy — all of it is Kingdom territory, all of it is lived under the reign of God, all of it is offered to the King who has already given everything to bring his people into his Kingdom.

Seek first his kingdom. Not alongside everything else. First. Because when the Kingdom is first, "all these things" — the daily provisions, the anxieties that Matthew 6 has just been addressing — find their right place. Not as the centre of life but as the gifts of the King who has already given more than any of them are worth.
