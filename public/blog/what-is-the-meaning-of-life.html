LIFE & PURPOSE

What Is the Meaning of Life? What the Bible Says

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

The philosopher who asks this question from a lecture hall is asking it differently from the person who asks it at 2am after achieving everything they were told would make them happy and finding themselves in the same hollow place they started from. This article is written for the second person.

Because the Bible's answer to the meaning of life is not a philosophical proposition. It is an encounter with a person. And it begins, unexpectedly, not with the answer but with the most honest acknowledgment in ancient literature that achievement alone cannot provide what the question is looking for.

The Diagnosis — Vanity of Vanities

Ecclesiastes 1:2 — "Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity."

The Hebrew word translated "vanity" is hebel — literally, breath or vapour. Something real but insubstantial. Something you can feel but cannot hold. Something that exists and then is gone. The Preacher is not saying that life is worthless. He is saying that every attempt to find ultimate meaning in the things that life contains — apart from the one who gave it — produces the experience of grasping at mist.

The Preacher tries everything. Pleasure — "I denied myself nothing my eyes desired; I refused my heart no pleasure" (2:10). Work — "I undertook great projects" (2:4). Wisdom — "I applied myself to the understanding of wisdom" (1:17). Wealth, achievement, position, legacy. And the verdict after all of it: "Yet when I surveyed all that my hands had done and what I had toiled to achieve, everything was meaningless, a chasing after the wind" (2:11).

The reason the Preacher's diagnosis still resonates three thousand years later is that the human experience has not changed. Every generation produces people who achieve what they were told would satisfy them and discover that it does not. The salary reached, the relationship formed, the status achieved — and the same hollow question still present underneath it all. The Preacher is not a pessimist. He is the most honest voice in the room.

But he is not the final word.

Made for Relationship — The New Testament Answer

The New Testament does not answer the emptiness of Ecclesiastes with a better achievement. It answers it with a person.

Augustine of Hippo, writing in the fourth century, put the biblical answer in its most famous formulation: "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you." This is not a pious sentiment. It is an anthropological claim: the human being was made with a specific orientation, and that orientation is toward God. The restlessness that the Preacher described — the experience of grasping at vapour — is what happens when the created thing is oriented toward anything other than the one for whom it was made.

Genesis 1:27 — "So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them." The image of God — the imago Dei — is the definition of what a human being is. Not a biological organism that developed consciousness. Not an economic unit. Not a social animal. A creature made in the image of God — which means designed for relationship with God, bearing something of his character, oriented toward something that exceeds the material world.

The Fall did not eliminate the imago Dei. It distorted it — so that instead of the human being oriented toward God, the human being is oriented toward everything else, looking for in the creation what can only be found in the Creator. The Preacher's hebel — the vapour-experience of grasping at the things that cannot finally satisfy — is the experience of the imago Dei looking for God in the wrong places.

Made in the Image of Christ — The Fuller Picture

The New Testament takes the imago Dei further than Genesis does. Romans 8:29 — "For those God foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brothers and sisters."

The destiny of the human being is not simply to bear a generic image of God. It is to be conformed to the specific image of Christ — the one who is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15), the perfect human who lived the life every human was designed for. Jesus is not only the Saviour. He is the pattern — the model of what a human being fully oriented toward God looks like in practice.

2 Corinthians 3:18 — "We all, who with unveiled faces contemplate the Lord's glory, are being transformed into his image with ever-increasing glory, which comes from the Lord, who is the Spirit." Transformed into his image — progressively, by the Spirit, from one degree of glory to the next. The meaning of life, in the New Testament, is not a destination to arrive at. It is a transformation to undergo — the ongoing conforming of the believer to the image of the one for whom they were made.

This transformation is not self-improvement. It is not the human being working hard to become more Christ-like through disciplined effort. It is the work of the Spirit — the one who was poured out at Pentecost to do from the inside what no external law could produce. Galatians 5:22-23 — the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. This is not a checklist. It is the shape of the life that emerges when a person is genuinely oriented toward God through Christ in the Spirit.

Made for Worship — The Destination of Human Existence

Revelation 4:11 — "You are worthy, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honour and power, for you created all things, and by your will they were created and have their being." The elders around the throne cast their crowns before the one who sits on it — and they do not resent the casting. They do it again. And again. The casting of the crowns — the laying down of every achievement, every status, every thing that could be grasped at as a source of worth — is the act that finally produces what all the Preacher's achievements never could: genuine, sustainable, unending joy.

C.S. Lewis made the connection between praising and enjoying: "I think we delight to praise what we enjoy because the praise not merely expresses but completes the enjoyment; it is its appointed consummation." The meaning of life, in the biblical picture, is not a philosophical category. It is a relational activity: the creature delighting in the Creator, praising what is genuinely, permanently worthy of praise, and in that praise finding the fullness that everything else promised and could not deliver.

John 4:23-24 — "Yet a time is coming and has now come when the true worshippers will worship the Father in the Spirit and in truth, for they are the kind of worshippers the Father seeks." The Father seeks worshippers. Not achievers. Not performers. Not those who have accumulated enough to no longer need him. Worshippers — those who have found in God what everything else was merely pointing toward.

This is the meaning of life. Not a what. A who. The God who made you for himself, who sent his Son to bring you back from the far country of hebel, who poured out his Spirit to begin the transformation into the image of the Son — and who is, in his own eternal being, the love, the joy, the beauty, and the life for which the human heart was always restless.

The New Creation — A Future That Gives the Present Meaning

One more dimension that the Bible adds — and that no secular account of meaning can provide. The meaning of life is not undermined by death. It is completed beyond it.

Romans 8:18 — "I consider that our present sufferings are not worth comparing with the glory that will be revealed in us." The present — with all its pain, its loss, its unfinished questions — is not the whole story. It is the preface. The glory coming is so disproportionate to the suffering present that Paul cannot find a comparison worth making.

Revelation 21:4-5 — "He will wipe every tear from their eyes. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the old order of things has passed away... I am making everything new." Making everything new — not making new things, but making the things that exist new. The creation restored, the image fully recovered, the relationship finally unhindered.

The person who lives with the knowledge of this future has a resource against meaninglessness that no achievement can provide. The losses of the present are real — but they are not final. The incompleteness of the present is real — but it will not be the last word. The meaning of life includes but is not limited to what can be experienced in a single human lifetime. It reaches into a future that death cannot touch.
