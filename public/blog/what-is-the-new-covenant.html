What Is the New Covenant? How It Changes Everything

By Sir Henry M M  ·  In Him Daily

What Is the New Covenant? How It Changes Everything

The question "am I still under the law?" is asked by more Christians than will admit it. Not always in those exact words — but in the anxiety that says: have I prayed enough this week? Have I been faithful enough? Have I given enough? Is God pleased with me right now, in this specific moment, or is he waiting for me to improve before full relationship is available?

That anxiety is the experience of living under the wrong covenant. And the answer to it is not try harder. The answer is understand what the New Covenant actually is — because when you understand it, the anxiety does not merely diminish. It loses its foundation.

What a Covenant Is

Before tracing the biblical covenants, it helps to understand what a covenant is. A covenant is not a contract. A contract is a mutual agreement between roughly equal parties, conditional on performance from both sides. If one party fails, the contract can be voided.

A covenant in the biblical sense is something different: a binding commitment made by a superior to an inferior, often sealed by sacrifice, that defines the terms of the relationship going forward. The biblical covenants are not negotiated agreements. They are sovereign declarations — God committing himself to specific relationships with specific people under specific terms.

The sequence of biblical covenants moves in a consistent direction: toward greater grace, greater access, and more permanent relationship. Each covenant does not replace the previous one randomly. It builds toward a culmination that the previous covenants were always anticipating.

The Covenant With Abraham — Promise Before Performance

The foundational covenant of the Old Testament is the covenant God made with Abraham in Genesis 12 and sealed in Genesis 15. It contains three promises: land, descendants, and blessing — specifically, that through Abraham all nations of the earth would be blessed.

Genesis 15 records the covenant ratification ceremony — a remarkable event in which God causes a deep sleep to fall on Abraham, and the divine presence alone passes between the divided animal carcasses. In the ancient Near East, covenant ceremonies involved both parties walking between divided animals — a self-imprecatory oath meaning "may what happened to these animals happen to me if I break this covenant." In Genesis 15, Abraham does not walk between the animals. God alone does. The covenant is unilateral — its fulfilment depends entirely on God, not on Abraham's subsequent performance.

Galatians 3:16-17 makes the New Testament point explicit: "The promises were spoken to Abraham and to his seed... meaning one person, who is Christ. What I mean is this: the law, introduced 430 years later, does not set aside the covenant previously established by God and thus do away with the promise." The Abrahamic covenant was promise-based, grace-rooted, and not nullified by the later giving of the law.

The Mosaic Covenant — Law and Its Limitations

The covenant God made with Israel through Moses at Sinai is different in character from the Abrahamic covenant. It is conditional: "If you fully obey the Lord your God and carefully follow all his commands... the Lord your God will set you high above all the nations on earth" (Deuteronomy 28:1). If Israel obeyed, blessing. If Israel disobeyed, curse. The blessings and curses of Deuteronomy 28 are the terms.

The law of the Mosaic covenant was not arbitrary. Romans 7:12 — "the law is holy, and the commandment is holy, righteous and good." It revealed the character of God and the standard of holiness. It exposed sin by naming it. Galatians 3:24 — "the law was our guardian until Christ came that we might be justified by faith."

But the Mosaic covenant had a limitation built into it — not a flaw but a design feature that pointed beyond itself. Hebrews 10:1 — "the law is only a shadow of the good things that are coming — not the realities themselves. For this reason it can never, by the same sacrifices repeated endlessly year after year, make perfect those who draw near to worship." The sacrifices had to be repeated. The high priest had to enter the Most Holy Place every year on the Day of Atonement. The law could show the standard but could not produce the inner transformation needed to meet it. The Mosaic covenant was always pointing forward.

Jeremiah 31 — The Promise of the New Covenant

The most explicit Old Testament prophecy of the New Covenant appears in Jeremiah 31:31-34. It was written during one of the darkest periods in Israel's history — the impending fall of Jerusalem and the exile to Babylon. And it contains the most extraordinary promise in the Old Testament:

"'The days are coming,' declares the Lord, 'when I will make a new covenant with the people of Israel and with the people of Judah. It will not be like the covenant I made with their ancestors when I took them by the hand to lead them out of Egypt, because they broke my covenant, though I was a husband to them,' declares the Lord. 'This is the covenant I will make with the people of Israel after that time,' declares the Lord. 'I will put my law in their minds and write it on their hearts. I will be their God, and they will be my people. No longer will they teach their neighbour, or say to one another, "Know the Lord," because they will all know me, from the least of them to the greatest,' declares the Lord. 'For I will forgive their wickedness and will remember their sins no more.'"

Four features of the New Covenant announced here. First: it is explicitly different from the Mosaic covenant — "not like the covenant I made with their ancestors." Second: the law is written on the heart, not on stone tablets — the transformation is internal, not external. Third: direct personal knowledge of God is available to everyone, from the least to the greatest — not mediated through a priestly class. Fourth: forgiveness is complete and final — "I will remember their sins no more."

Hebrews 8-10 — The New Covenant Arrived

The letter to the Hebrews is the New Testament's fullest exposition of the New Covenant — and it quotes Jeremiah 31 in full in Hebrews 8, the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament. The argument of Hebrews 8-10 is built around five "betters":

Better mediator. Hebrews 8:6 — "the ministry Jesus has received is as superior to theirs as the covenant of which he is mediator is superior to the old one, since the new covenant is established on better promises." Moses mediated between God and Israel at Sinai. Jesus mediates between God and humanity — not as a human representative approaching a distant deity, but as the God-Man who is simultaneously fully divine and fully human, able to represent both sides of the relationship perfectly.

Better sacrifice. Hebrews 9:12 — "he did not enter by means of the blood of goats and calves; but he entered the Most Holy Place once for all by his own blood, thus obtaining eternal redemption." The annual Day of Atonement sacrifice of the Mosaic covenant required repetition because it could not permanently address sin. Christ's sacrifice was once for all — ephapax — not to be repeated, not to be added to, permanently sufficient.

Better access. Hebrews 10:19-20 — "we have confidence to enter the Most Holy Place by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way opened for us through the curtain, that is, his body." Under the Mosaic covenant, only the high priest could enter the Most Holy Place, only once a year, only with sacrificial blood. Under the New Covenant, every believer has direct, confident access to the presence of the Father — not once a year, but always, because the way was opened by Christ and remains open.

Better promises. The Mosaic covenant promised land, national blessing, and physical prosperity contingent on obedience. The New Covenant promises forgiveness, the indwelling Spirit, direct knowledge of God, and eternal life — promises that do not expire and are not contingent on the performance of those who receive them.

Better covenant. Hebrews 8:13 — "By calling this covenant 'new,' he has made the first one obsolete; and what is obsolete and ageing will soon disappear." The New Covenant does not supplement the Mosaic covenant. It replaces it — not by invalidating its witness to God's character, but by fulfilling what it was always pointing toward and providing what it could not provide.

Am I Still Under the Law?

Romans 6:14 — "sin shall no longer be your master, because you are not under the law, but under grace." The believer is not under the Mosaic covenant. They are under the New Covenant — the covenant of grace, mediated by Christ, sealed by his blood, secured by his permanent intercession.

This does not mean the moral content of the law is irrelevant to the believer. Romans 8:3-4 — "what the law was powerless to do because it was weakened by the flesh, God did by sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh to be a sin offering. And so he condemned sin in the flesh, in order that the righteous requirement of the law might be fully met in us, who do not live according to the flesh but according to the Spirit."

The righteous requirement of the law is met in us — not by our performance of the law but by the Spirit who produces from the inside what the law required from the outside. The New Covenant believer does not obey the law to earn God's approval. They are loved and approved freely, under the New Covenant, and the Spirit who indwells them produces the character that the law described.

The anxiety — have I done enough? Is God pleased with me? — loses its foundation under the New Covenant. Because the New Covenant's terms do not depend on the believer's performance. They depend on Christ's. And his performance is perfect and permanent. Hebrews 10:14 — "by one sacrifice he has made perfect forever those who are being made holy."

How the New Covenant Changes How We Read the Bible

The New Covenant is not just a doctrinal category. It is a hermeneutical key — a lens that changes how every passage of the Old Testament should be read.

Every Old Testament sacrifice points to the one sufficient sacrifice. Every priest points to the one perfect mediator. Every promise of land and blessing points to the spiritual inheritance that exceeds the physical. Every law written on stone points to the law written on the heart by the Spirit. Every Day of Atonement points to the once-for-all atonement. The Old Testament, read through the New Covenant lens, is not a different religion from Christianity. It is the same story at an earlier stage — with every element pointing toward the fulfilment that the New Covenant delivers.

Luke 22:20 — at the Last Supper, Jesus lifted the cup and said: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you." The new covenant was ratified not with the blood of animals at Sinai but with the blood of the mediator himself. And every time the Lord's Supper is celebrated, it is the New Covenant community proclaiming the death and resurrection of the one who sealed the covenant they are living in.
