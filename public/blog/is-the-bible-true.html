Is the Bible True? Can We Trust It?

By Sir Henry M M  ·  In Him Daily

Is the Bible True? Can We Trust It?

The question "is the Bible true?" is asked from many different starting places. Some people ask it from a place of genuine intellectual enquiry — wanting to know whether the documents that claim to be the word of God have any reliable basis. Some ask it from a place of specific doubt — a particular passage that seems historically questionable, or a contradiction they cannot reconcile. Some ask it having just been told by someone they respect that the Bible cannot be trusted.

All of these are legitimate starting places. And all of them deserve honest engagement rather than defensive dismissal or uncritical assertion.

This article addresses the evidence — manuscript, archaeological, internal, and prophetic — without turning it into a legal brief. Then it makes the claim that no amount of evidence alone can make: the Bible is not primarily a document to be authenticated. It is the place where a person is encountered.

The Manuscript Evidence — How We Got the Bible We Have

Before asking whether the Bible is true, it is worth asking whether the Bible we have is the Bible that was written. Scribes copying documents by hand across centuries inevitably introduced variations. How many variations? How significant? And how does the Bible compare with other ancient documents?

The New Testament has more manuscript evidence than any other ancient document by a vast margin. There are approximately 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, plus over 10,000 Latin manuscripts and many thousands more in other languages — totalling over 24,000 manuscript copies of portions of the New Testament. For comparison, Julius Caesar's Gallic Wars is supported by around 10 manuscripts. Plato's works by about 210. Homer's Iliad — the best-supported ancient work after the New Testament — by around 1,900.

The time gap between the original writing and the earliest surviving copies is also crucial. For most ancient documents, that gap is hundreds of years. For the New Testament, the Rylands Papyrus — a fragment of John 18 — dates to approximately AD 125, within decades of John's Gospel being written. The Chester Beatty Papyri contain large portions of the New Testament and date to approximately AD 200.

When textual scholars compare all these manuscripts, they find that approximately 99% of the New Testament can be reconstructed with confidence, and that none of the variations affect any core Christian doctrine. The New Testament we have is reliably the New Testament that was written.

The Old Testament manuscript situation was dramatically confirmed by the Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered between 1947 and 1956. These scrolls, copied approximately 100 BC, contain portions of every Old Testament book except Esther. The Isaiah Scroll — a complete copy of Isaiah — is a thousand years older than any previously known Hebrew manuscript of Isaiah and matches the text we have with remarkable accuracy. The documents had been faithfully transmitted across a millennium.

Archaeological Corroboration — What the Ground Confirms

Archaeology has consistently confirmed the historical reliability of the biblical narrative at a level that surprises many sceptics. This does not mean archaeology proves every theological claim of the Bible — it cannot. But it does mean that the historical setting, the named people, the described places, and the recorded events of the Bible have been corroborated at a rate that would not be expected from purely fictional or legendary documents.

A small selection of significant finds:

The Pool of Siloam (John 9:7) was long treated by sceptics as a theological construct. Archaeologists discovered it in Jerusalem in 2004 — exactly where John described it, with the architecture consistent with the first-century dating of the Gospel.

Pontius Pilate was known only from the Gospels and a brief mention in Tacitus until 1961, when the Pilate Stone was discovered at Caesarea Maritima — a limestone block bearing the inscription: "Pontius Pilatus, Prefect of Judaea." The governor who sentenced Jesus to death was confirmed as a historical person with the exact title the Gospels give him.

The Hezekiah Tunnel in Jerusalem — described in 2 Kings 20:20 — was discovered in the nineteenth century, complete with an inscription from the workmen who dug it describing exactly how they dug from both ends and met in the middle, precisely as the historical narrative would require.

The Moabite Stone, the Tel Dan Stele (which refers explicitly to "the house of David"), the Cyrus Cylinder (confirming the Cyrus decree recorded in Ezra 1), and numerous other artefacts have corroborated specific biblical claims that were previously dismissed as legendary.

The pattern is consistent: where the biblical narrative makes specific historical claims that can be tested archaeologically, those claims have repeatedly been confirmed. This does not prove that the Bible is divinely inspired. But it does establish that it is not careless about historical detail.

Internal Consistency — One Story Across Sixty-Six Books

The Bible was written by approximately forty different authors, over approximately 1,500 years, in three different languages, from vastly different cultural contexts — kings, fishermen, shepherds, priests, a physician, a tax collector, a military general. And it tells one coherent story with one coherent central character moving through it from beginning to end.

This internal coherence is remarkable and requires explanation. The themes of covenant, sacrifice, redemption, the coming King, the suffering servant, and the restoration of all things are woven through documents written centuries apart by people who could not have coordinated their testimony. The New Testament's use of the Old Testament is not the grafting of a new story onto an unrelated foundation — it is the declaration that the same story has arrived at its destination.

Jesus himself made this claim on the Emmaus Road (Luke 24:44): "Everything must be fulfilled that is written about me in the Law of Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms." He is not claiming that isolated proof texts predict isolated events. He is claiming that the entire Old Testament — all three sections of the Hebrew canon — tells a coherent story that he is the climax of.

The unity of the Bible across its diversity is either an extraordinary coincidence, a conspiracy of unprecedented scale, or evidence that the documents share a common divine source. Of these options, the third is the most parsimonious.

The Prophecy Argument — Specific Claims Made Before the Events

The Old Testament contains hundreds of specific predictive prophecies — claims made about future events that were subsequently fulfilled in historically verifiable ways. The sheer number and specificity of these prophecies is among the strongest arguments for the divine origin of the Scriptures.

Isaiah 44:28 and 45:1 name Cyrus — by name, approximately 150 years before he was born — as the king who would allow the exiled Israelites to return and rebuild Jerusalem. The Cyrus Cylinder, discovered in 1879, confirms that Cyrus issued exactly the decree Isaiah predicted. The sceptical response — that these chapters of Isaiah were written after Cyrus, not before — requires substantial reinterpretation of the book's own internal evidence and the Dead Sea Scrolls confirmation of the unified text.

Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem as the birthplace of the coming ruler of Israel more than seven centuries before the birth of Jesus there. Daniel 9:24-27 describes a timeline for the coming of the anointed one that, when calculated from the decree to rebuild Jerusalem (confirmed in Nehemiah), arrives at approximately the time of Jesus' ministry and death. Zechariah 9:9 describes the king coming into Jerusalem riding on a donkey — fulfilled in the Palm Sunday entry described in all four Gospels.

The cumulative weight of prophecy fulfilled is not the result of vague prediction that could fit many outcomes. It involves specific named people, specific named places, and specific described events — in documents whose pre-event dating is established by manuscript evidence.

The Personal Claim — Beyond Evidence to Encounter

The evidence above establishes that the Bible is historically reliable, archaeologically corroborated, internally coherent, and prophetically remarkable. What it cannot establish — and what no external evidence can establish — is the claim the Bible makes about itself: that it is the living word of God, through which God speaks to the people who receive it.

2 Timothy 3:16 — "All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness." The word "God-breathed" — theopneustos — is not a claim about mechanical dictation. It is a claim about divine origin and ongoing vitality. The breath of God is in the text. And the text that carries the divine breath does something that authenticated ancient documents do not do: it changes the person who receives it.

Hebrews 4:12 — "For the word of God is alive and active. Sharper than any double-edged sword, it penetrates even to dividing soul and spirit, joints and marrow; it judges the thoughts and attitudes of the heart." The word is not a historical artifact to be examined. It is a living agent that examines the person holding it.

Jesus himself referred to the Scriptures as the testimony to him (John 5:39) — not a set of propositions to be believed but the place where he is found. The primary question the Bible invites is not "is this document reliable?" — though that question matters and has been answered. The primary question is "who is this person that the document points to?" And that question is answered not by further historical investigation but by the personal encounter that happens when the Bible's central figure becomes specific and personal rather than abstract and historical.

The Bible can be trusted. The evidence for that is substantial. But trust based only on evidence is not the trust the Bible is designed to produce. It is designed to produce the trust of the person who has met the one it speaks of — and found him to be exactly what the book says he is.
