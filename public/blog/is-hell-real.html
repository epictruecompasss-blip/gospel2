Is Hell Real? What Jesus Said About It

By Sir Henry M M  ·  In Him Daily

Is Hell Real? What Jesus Said About It

No doctrine of Christianity is more avoided in preaching, more dismissed in culture, and more misrepresented in both directions — either sensationalised into a torture chamber designed to terrorise, or quietly abandoned as incompatible with a loving God — than hell.

This article neither sensationalises nor abandons it. It traces what the Bible actually says — specifically, what Jesus said — and addresses the question honestly from the one who spoke more about hell than any other figure in the New Testament.

The Most Important Observation About Hell

Before examining what the Bible says about hell, the most important observation is this: the person who spoke about hell most extensively in the entire New Testament was not a thundering prophet of judgment. It was Jesus.

Jesus referred to hell more than any other New Testament figure. More than Paul. More than John. More than any of the apostles. And he spoke about it not to terrify but to warn — the way a physician describes the consequence of an untreated disease, not to frighten the patient into submission but because the disease is real and the treatment is available.

The person most concerned about people going to hell was Jesus. Which is the precise reason he came.

What the Bible Actually Says

The New Testament uses several words that English translations often render as "hell." Understanding the distinctions matters.

Hades is the realm of the dead — the intermediate state between death and the final resurrection and judgment. Luke 16:23 places the rich man in Hades after death, where he is in torment. Acts 2:31 says Christ's soul was not left in Hades at the resurrection. Hades is temporary — it delivers up its dead at the final judgment (Revelation 20:13) and is itself thrown into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:14).

Gehenna is the word Jesus used most frequently for the place of final judgment — usually translated "hell" in English. It is the Greek form of the Hebrew ge-hinnom — the Valley of Hinnom, a ravine outside Jerusalem that had been the site of child sacrifice in the Old Testament and became, in Jesus' day, the city's rubbish dump where fires burned continuously. Jesus used this geography-laden word as the image for the place of final punishment — a place of destruction, fire, and irreversibility.

The lake of fire appears in Revelation 19-20 as the final destination of Satan, the beast, the false prophet, death, Hades, and "anyone whose name was not found written in the book of life" (Revelation 20:15). It is described as "the second death" — not the cessation of existence but the permanent separation from life as God defines it.

What Jesus Said

Matthew 10:28 — "Do not be afraid of those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Rather, be afraid of the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell." The fear Jesus recommends is not fear of death — physical death is within human power to inflict. It is fear of the One who has authority over the final state of the whole person after death. The seriousness of that authority is the reason the warning matters.

Matthew 25:41, 46 — "Then he will say to those on his left, 'Depart from me, you who are cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels'... Then they will go away to eternal punishment, but the righteous to eternal life." The same Greek word — aionios (eternal) — is used for both eternal punishment and eternal life in this passage. If eternal life is genuinely unending, then eternal punishment is described with the same qualifier.

Mark 9:43-48 — "If your hand causes you to stumble, cut it off. It is better for you to enter life maimed than with two hands to go into hell, where the fire never goes out... where the worms that eat them do not die, and the fire is not quenched." Jesus uses language drawn from Isaiah 66:24 — the image of the consequences of final rebellion against God. The hyperbole about cutting off hands and feet establishes the severity of the alternative. Whatever hell is, it is worse than any earthly loss.

Luke 16:19-31 — the parable of the rich man and Lazarus. The rich man, in Hades after death, is in torment. He can see Lazarus in Abraham's side but cannot cross the great chasm fixed between them. He cannot reach Lazarus. Lazarus cannot come to him. And the request to warn his brothers is answered with: "they have Moses and the Prophets; let them listen to them." The warning is available. The Word of God is the warning. The time to receive it is now.

The Three Main Views — Presented Fairly

Three serious positions have been held by Bible-believing Christians on the nature of hell. Each takes the biblical text seriously. Each produces different pastoral implications.

Eternal conscious torment — the traditional view held by the majority of Christian tradition. Those who die apart from Christ experience ongoing, conscious suffering separated from God eternally. The "eternal" of Matthew 25:46 is read as genuinely unending. The fire and torment imagery of Revelation is taken as describing ongoing experience.

Annihilationism (conditional immortality) — the view that the unsaved do not experience conscious torment eternally but are ultimately destroyed — cease to exist entirely. Proponents point to language of "destruction" (Matthew 10:28, 2 Thessalonians 1:9), "perishing" (John 3:16), and "the second death" (Revelation 20:14). They argue that immortality is a gift given to believers, not an inherent property of all souls.

Universalism — the view that all people will ultimately be saved, perhaps through a process of purification after death. This view is the minority position among serious biblical scholars and requires substantial reinterpretation of the Matthew 25:46 parallel between eternal life and eternal punishment, and the "great chasm fixed" of Luke 16:26.

This article does not claim to settle the debate between eternal conscious torment and annihilationism — both have been held by serious, Bible-honouring theologians. Universalism is addressed and gently declined on the basis of the same word (aionios) being used for the duration of both eternal life and eternal punishment in Matthew 25:46.

What all three positions agree on is this: the consequences of dying outside of Christ are serious, irreversible, and something Jesus warned about more than anyone else in the New Testament.

How Can a Loving God Send People to Hell?

This is the objection that makes hell feel incompatible with the character of God. And it deserves a direct answer.

The first thing to say is that in the Bible's picture, God does not send people to hell in the way that a judge arbitrarily assigns punishment. Hell is, at its root, the end state of the person who has persistently and finally chosen against God — who has refused the relationship, declined the invitation, rejected the forgiveness that was freely offered.

C.S. Lewis put it most memorably: "There are only two kinds of people in the end: those who say to God, 'Thy will be done,' and those to whom God says, in the end, 'Thy will be done.'" Hell is, in this sense, the honouring of a choice — the ultimate destination of the person who has chosen themselves over God and found, at the end of all things, what that choice actually means without God present to mitigate it.

The love of God and the existence of hell are not incompatible. They require each other. A God who created human beings as genuinely free agents — capable of real relationship, real love, real choice — must honour the freedom that makes those things possible. Love that cannot be refused is not love. The door of hell is, as Lewis suggested, locked from the inside.

The second thing to say is that the love of God's response to the reality of hell is the cross. The God who is most concerned about people going there sent his Son to provide the way out. John 3:16 — "God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life." The alternative to perishing is not achieving enough to avoid it. It is believing — receiving what has been freely given.

The Urgency of the Warning

Jesus spoke about hell more than anyone else in the New Testament because he came to prevent it. The warning is not the message. The warning points to the message: there is a way out, there is a door open, there is one whose arms are wide.

Luke 15:4 — "Suppose one of you has a hundred sheep and loses one of them. Doesn't he leave the ninety-nine in the open country and go after the lost sheep until he finds it?" The shepherd who goes after the lost sheep is the God who comes after the one who is heading toward what the warning describes. The warning exists because the rescue is available.

If hell is real — and Jesus believed it was real enough to warn about repeatedly and to die on a cross to provide an alternative — then the most loving thing anyone can do is say so, clearly and without either sensationalism or evasion, and then point to the one who is the alternative.
