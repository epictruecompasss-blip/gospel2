Why Do Bad Things Happen to Good People? A Biblical Response

By Sir Henry M M  ·  In Him Daily

Why Do Bad Things Happen to Good People? A Biblical Response

The title of this article is a book title — Rabbi Harold Kushner wrote the famous version in 1981, after the death of his son. But the question is older than any book. It appears in the oldest poetry in the Bible. It has been asked by every person who has ever prayed sincerely and then watched things fall apart anyway.

"I prayed. I trusted. And it fell apart. Where was God?"

This article does not give you the answer that resolves the question. There is no such answer that satisfies a person in genuine grief. What this article does is go to the Bible's most honest engagement with the question — and discover that the God of the Bible is not threatened by the asking.

Why We Need to Get the Question Right First

The title "why do bad things happen to good people?" contains an assumption worth examining: that the distribution of suffering in the world should track moral performance. Good people should get good things. Bad people should get bad things. When bad things happen to good people, the distribution has malfunctioned and God — if he exists and if he is good — is responsible for the malfunction.

This assumption is natural. It feels self-evident. And the Bible does not simply dismiss it. But the Bible does complicate it — in two specific ways.

First: there are no entirely good people. Romans 3:23 — "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." The category "good people" in the absolute moral sense required by the assumption is empty. Every human being is a mixed person — capable of genuine goodness and genuine evil, carrying both in the same life. The question of why bad things happen to good people often contains, on examination, a comparison between the apparent goodness of one person and the apparent badness of what happened to them. But the comparison is always between a person's surface presentation and their experience, not between their actual moral status and an expectation of protection.

Second, and more importantly: the assumption that good behaviour should produce protection from suffering is exactly the assumption that the book of Job was written to challenge.

Job — The Man Who Lost Everything and Was Told He Must Have Deserved It

Job 1:1 — "In the land of Uz there lived a man whose name was Job. This man was blameless and upright; he feared God and shunned evil." The narrator's introduction of Job is unambiguous. He is not introduced as a man with hidden sins that will eventually be revealed as the cause of his suffering. He is introduced as blameless and upright. God himself says the same thing in Job 1:8.

And then Job loses everything. His livestock. His servants. His children. His health. In rapid succession, without any explanation given to him, the life he had built and the family he had loved and the body he inhabited are all stripped away. He sits in ash, scraping his sores with broken pottery.

His three friends arrive. And they bring the assumption that the question of this article rests on: that suffering tracks moral failure. Eliphaz: "Who, being innocent, has ever perished? Where were the upright ever destroyed?" (Job 4:7). Bildad: "Does God pervert justice? Does the Almighty pervert what is right? When your children sinned against him, he gave them over to the penalty of their sin" (8:3-4). Zophar: "Know this: God has even forgotten some of your sin" (11:6). The argument of all three is the same — you are suffering, therefore you sinned. Search your heart. Find the hidden fault. Confess it. Then the suffering will make sense.

Job refuses. Not because he claims perfection — he knows he is not perfect. But because the equation his friends are drawing does not match his experience or his understanding of God. And he demands that God answer him.

What God says when he finally speaks (Job 38-41) is not what anyone expected. He does not explain why Job suffered. He does not give the backstory of the heavenly council. He does not apologise. He asks Job a series of questions about the creation — where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Have you entered the springs of the sea? Can you bind the Pleiades? — question after question that establishes not that Job's suffering was deserved but that the universe is larger and more complex than Job's framework for it.

And then — the most significant verse in the book — God's verdict on the whole exchange: Job 42:7 — "After the Lord had said these things to Job, he said to Eliphaz the Temanite, 'I am angry with you and your two friends, because you have not spoken the truth about me, as my servant Job has.'" The friends who defended God with theological explanations for Job's suffering were wrong. Job, who argued with God and demanded an answer, was right. God was angrier with the tidy theology than with the raw complaint.

What God's Answer to Job Actually Is

God's answer to Job is not an explanation. It is a presence.

Job had spent thirty-five chapters demanding that God appear and answer. And when God appeared in the whirlwind, the questions he asked Job were not answers in the conventional sense. They were the revelation of a God who is larger than any framework Job had available — larger than the suffering, larger than the theology of retribution, larger than anything Job's friends had tried to contain him in.

And something remarkable happened: Job was satisfied. Job 42:5 — "My ears had heard of you but now my eyes have seen you." The suffering was not explained. The losses were not reversed (they were later restored, but that is a separate point). But Job encountered God — directly, personally, in the whirlwind — and that encounter was the answer. Not to the question "why" but to the deeper question beneath it: "Is God there? Does he know? Does he care?"

He was there. He knew. He cared enough to speak.

Psalm 23:4 — "Even though I walk through the darkest valley, I will fear no evil, for you are with me." The reason for the absence of fear is not the absence of the valley. The psalmist is walking through the darkest valley — through it, not around it, not spared from it. The reason for the absence of fear is the presence: for you are with me. The answer to the valley is not a map around it. It is a companion through it.

The Cross — God's Entrance Into the Suffering

The Old Testament book of Job establishes that God refuses to be contained in the theology of retribution — that bad things happening to good people is not a malfunction of a system designed to reward goodness. But the New Testament goes further.

The cross is God's answer to suffering in the most unexpected possible form: God himself suffers.

Philippians 2:6-8 — the Son of God "did not consider equality with God something to be used to his own advantage; rather, he made himself nothing, taking the very nature of a servant, being made in human likeness. And being found in appearance as a man, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to death — even death on a cross."

The God who made the world did not stay outside the suffering his world contained. He entered it — fully, without the divine protection that could have been available — and experienced from the inside what he had been watching from the outside: betrayal, abandonment, physical agony, and the experience of forsakenness. Matthew 27:46 — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" The cry from the cross is the cry of genuine experienced abandonment — not a performance, not a metaphor. The eternal Son of God experienced what it is to be without the Father.

Hebrews 4:15 — "we do not have a high priest who is unable to empathise with our weaknesses, but we have one who has been tempted in every way, just as we are — yet he did not sin." The high priest who intercedes for us is not interceding from the comfort of untouched divine distance. He intercedes as one who entered the specific suffering that characterises human existence — including its most extreme forms.

This does not explain why suffering exists. But it permanently answers the suspicion that God is watching from a distance, unmoved. He entered it. He knows what it is from the inside. He is not explaining the suffering from outside. He is the one who went through it.

Caleb's Forty-Five Years — The Long Faithfulness

One of the most important biblical answers to the "where was God?" question is not a theological argument. It is a story.

Caleb was one of the twelve spies sent into Canaan in Numbers 13. He and Joshua returned with a faithful report. The other ten returned with fear. The community chose the fear. And because of that collective failure, Caleb — who had been faithful — spent the next forty-five years in the wilderness with a generation of faithless people.

He did not deserve those forty-five years. His faithfulness should, by the theology of retribution, have protected him from the consequence of others' faithlessness. It did not. He suffered the long wilderness alongside people whose faithlessness had produced it.

And then, at eighty-five years old, he stood before Joshua and said: "Give me this hill country that the Lord promised me that day" (Joshua 14:12). Forty-five years later. The promise was still alive in him. The faithfulness had not been erased by the delay. The forty-five years of apparently wasted wandering were the preparation for a mountain that a forty-year-old would not have had the specific faith to claim.

This does not answer why bad things happen to good people. But it suggests something: the delay is not the destruction. The valley is not the destination. The waiting that feels like abandonment may be the formation that the inheritance requires.

What to Do With the Question That Has No Answer

Some suffering — perhaps most suffering — will not be explained in this lifetime. Job never learned why. The forty-five years in the wilderness were never explained to Caleb. The cross appeared to be a catastrophic failure on Friday before it became the defining victory on Sunday.

The Bible's invitation in the face of unexplained suffering is not to the resolution of the question but to the relationship with the one who holds the answer. Psalm 62:8 — "Trust in him at all times, you people; pour out your hearts to him, for God is our refuge." Pour out your hearts — not tidy up your theology before approaching him. Pour out exactly what is in you. He is the refuge even when he is also the one being questioned.

And the promise that holds when nothing else does: Romans 8:28 — "we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him." Not: all things are good. Not: God caused all things. In all things — including the things that make no sense — God works for good. The working is active, ongoing, and anchored to the character of the one doing it, not to our ability to see it.
