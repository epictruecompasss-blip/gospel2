What Is Baptism? What the Bible Says and Why It Matters

Baptism is one of the most debated practices in Christianity — and one of the most misunderstood. Churches have divided over it. People have been told they are not properly Christian without it, or that it is merely an optional symbol with no real significance. Both extremes miss what the Bible actually says.

This article addresses the three questions most people are carrying when they search this topic: does baptism save you, what does it actually do, and what should you do if you were baptised as an infant and are now wondering whether to be baptised again as a believing adult?

What the Word Means

The Greek word baptizo means to immerse, to plunge, to dip — to submerge something in a liquid. In the ancient world it was used of dyeing cloth (plunging it into the dye until fully immersed), of a ship sinking (fully taken under the water), and of washing vessels. The word itself carries the image of going fully under.

This is not primarily an argument for immersion as the only valid mode — though it does explain why immersion was the dominant practice of the early church. It is the starting point for understanding what baptism is meant to signify. Something goes under. Something comes up. The going under and the coming up are the heart of the symbol.

What Baptism Is — The Biblical Picture

Romans 6:3-4 is the fullest theological statement about baptism in the New Testament: "Or don't you know that all of us who were baptised into Christ Jesus were baptised into his death? We were therefore buried with him through baptism into death in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may live a new life."

Three movements in this passage that define what baptism is:

Union with Christ's death. Baptism into Christ Jesus is baptism into his death — not merely association with him in a general sense, but identification with the specific event of his dying. The person who is baptised is declaring: his death is my death. What he bore at the cross, I have borne in him. The sin that required that death, and the condemnation that sin produced — I have died to it in him.

Burial. "We were buried with him through baptism into death." The burial element of baptism is deliberate and significant. Burial is the confirmation of death — the public, sealed, irreversible acknowledgment that the dying is complete. Going under the water is the burial: the old life, the old standing, the old orientation — sealed in the water.

Resurrection to new life. "We too may live a new life." Just as Christ was raised from the dead, the person coming up from the water comes up into a new life. Not the continuation of the old life with some modifications. A new life — the same continuity-in-transformation that the resurrection body has with the natural body. The person coming up from the water is the same person who went under — and fundamentally different.

Colossians 2:12 adds another dimension: "having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through your faith in the working of God, who raised him from the dead." The raised with him through faith — faith is the mechanism by which the union with Christ's resurrection is received. Baptism is the act. Faith is the receiving. The two are inseparable in the New Testament pattern.

Does Baptism Save You?

This is the most common question, and it requires precision because the answer is nuanced rather than simply yes or no.

Acts 2:38 — "Repent and be baptised, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins." Baptism is paired with repentance as the immediate response to the gospel. It is not separated from salvation as an optional later step. In the New Testament pattern, conversion and baptism happen together — not weeks or months apart.

Acts 22:16 — Ananias said to Paul: "And now what are you waiting for? Get up, be baptised and wash your sins away, calling on his name." The language "wash your sins away" is associated with baptism. 1 Peter 3:21 — "this water symbolises baptism that now saves you also — not the removal of dirt from the body but the pledge of a clear conscience toward God. It saves you by the resurrection of Jesus Christ."

These verses take baptism seriously as a saving act — not in the sense that the water itself has regenerative power, but in the sense that the act of baptism is the moment of public identification with Christ in which the spiritual realities of death and resurrection are experienced and declared.

The thief on the cross (Luke 23:43) is the clearest counter-evidence against baptismal regeneration in the mechanical sense — Jesus promised him paradise that day, with no opportunity for baptism. Salvation is received by faith. Baptism is the normal, expected, immediate expression of that faith — but not the exclusive mechanism through which salvation is received.

The most honest statement is this: in the New Testament, repentance and baptism and reception of the Spirit are presented as a unified experience — not three separate events to be spaced out across months. The question "does baptism save?" may be the wrong question. The better question is: what does the complete New Testament pattern of conversion look like? And the answer is: turning to Christ, being baptised into his name, and receiving the Spirit — together, as one movement of faith and response.

The Three Positions — Presented Fairly

Three serious positions exist within orthodox Christianity on baptism. Each takes Scripture seriously.

Believer's baptism (Baptist, Anabaptist, many evangelical traditions) — Only those who have personally and consciously repented and believed should be baptised. Baptism is the outward sign of an inward work already accomplished. Infant baptism is not valid because infants cannot exercise saving faith. Those baptised as infants who later come to faith should be baptised as believers.

The strongest arguments: the New Testament pattern consistently shows belief preceding baptism (Acts 2:41, 8:12, 8:36-38, 10:47-48, 16:14-15, 16:31-33). The word baptizo implies a conscious act. The commission of Matthew 28:19 says "make disciples... then baptise them" — discipleship precedes baptism.

Infant baptism / Paedobaptism (Reformed, Presbyterian, Lutheran, Anglican, Catholic traditions) — Baptism is the sign of the covenant — the New Covenant equivalent of circumcision, which was administered to infant boys in the Old Covenant. Households were baptised in Acts (Acts 16:15, 16:33, 1 Corinthians 1:16), which likely included infants. Infants are members of the covenant community.

The strongest arguments: Colossians 2:11-12 explicitly connects circumcision and baptism. The household baptisms of Acts are not qualified as adult-only. Children of believers are described as holy (1 Corinthians 7:14). The covenant community has always included children.

Sacramental baptism (Catholic, Orthodox, some Lutheran traditions) — Baptism is the means through which regeneration is ordinarily effected — the new birth is normally conferred in the act of baptism. 1 Peter 3:21, Acts 2:38, and John 3:5 are read as establishing baptism as the ordinary means of new birth.

This article does not claim to settle the debate between these positions. All three include serious, Scripture-honouring traditions. What all three agree on is this: baptism matters, it is connected to the death and resurrection of Christ, and it should not be treated as a trivial or optional religious formality.

The Circumcision at Gilgal — The Old Testament Type

Day 5 of the devotional series He Entered Before He Came traces the circumcision at Gilgal in Joshua 5:2-9. After crossing the Jordan, before the conquest of Jericho, all the men of Israel were circumcised — those who had been born in the wilderness and had never received the sign of the covenant.

Colossians 2:11-12 makes the typological connection explicit: "In him you were also circumcised with a circumcision not performed by human hands. Your whole self ruled by the flesh was put off when you were circumcised by Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him."

The circumcision at Gilgal happened between the Jordan crossing and the conquest. It was the act of covenant identification that came immediately after the people had crossed into the inheritance — after the crossing but before the conquest began. Baptism occupies the same structural position in the New Covenant life: after the crossing into the inheritance through faith, before the ongoing life of the inheritance is lived out. It marks the people as belonging to the covenant community, bearing the sign of the covenant in their body, identified as participants in what God has accomplished.

Can I Be Re-Baptised?

The question of re-baptism is asked most often by people baptised as infants who have since come to personal faith, or by people baptised in one tradition who are now part of a tradition that considers only believer's baptism valid.

There is no instance of re-baptism in the New Testament. Acts 19:1-7 is sometimes cited, but those disciples had received only John's baptism and had not yet heard of the Holy Spirit — a unique first-generation situation that does not directly address the infant-baptism question.

The pastoral answer to this question depends significantly on which theological tradition you are in and which understanding of baptism you hold. If you believe that baptism is the outward declaration of personal faith and you were baptised as an infant before you had personal faith, many serious theologians and pastors would say that being baptised as a believing adult is not re-baptism but the fulfilment of what the sign of baptism is meant to declare. If you believe that your infant baptism was your incorporation into the covenant community and that your personal faith is the maturing of what began there, you may not feel the need to be baptised again.

What matters most is not which tradition you are in but whether you have turned to Christ, received his forgiveness, and are living in the reality of the death and resurrection that baptism declares. The sign points to the reality. The reality is what the sign is for.
