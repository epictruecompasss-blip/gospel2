GRACE

What Is Grace? The Word That Changes Everything

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

Grace may be the most used and least understood word in Christianity. It appears in hymns, in sermons, in the names of churches and the titles of books. People say they believe in grace. And then they spend their lives trying to earn what grace has already given them.

This article is for the person who has heard about grace for years and still wakes up most mornings feeling like they have not done enough — that God is somewhere between disappointed and patient, waiting for them to improve sufficiently before full relationship is possible.

That person does not have a knowledge problem. They have a reception problem. They know about grace. They have not yet received it.

What the Word Actually Means

The New Testament word translated "grace" is the Greek charis. It appears over 150 times in the New Testament and carries a consistent meaning across every occurrence: unmerited, unearned, undeserved favour freely given.

Every part of that definition matters. Unmerited — there is no merit in the recipient that occasions it. Unearned — it cannot be obtained through effort or achievement. Undeserved — the recipient has no claim to it based on their record. Freely given — it comes from the character of the giver, not the condition of the receiver.

Romans 11:6 makes the definition explicit in a way that does not allow for compromise: "And if by grace, then it cannot be based on works; if it were, grace would no longer be grace." Paul is saying that grace and earning are mutually exclusive categories. The moment grace is made conditional on anything in the recipient — any achievement, any performance, any sufficient level of sincerity — it stops being grace. It becomes wages. And wages are owed, not given.

Grace is not the reward at the end of a process of improvement. It is not what God gives after you have demonstrated that you are serious about change. It is what God gives freely, entirely from his own character, to people who have no claim to it whatsoever.

Romans 5:8 — "But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." Not: while we were getting better. Not: while we were showing promise. While we were still sinners. Grace arrived before the improvement.

The Performance Trap — Why Knowing About Grace Is Not Enough

Here is the most common experience among people who claim to believe in grace: they believe it theologically but do not live from it experientially.

They know the doctrine. Grace means unmerited favour. They can define it. They can quote Ephesians 2:8. And then they live as if God's attitude toward them depends on how their quiet time went this morning, whether they lost their temper with their children, how consistently they gave, how sincerely they prayed, how many times they failed in the same area before finally getting it right.

This is what we can call the performance trap — and it is the most widespread condition in the church. It is not unbelief. It is a partial belief that has not yet become a full reception. The person in the performance trap believes that grace saved them. They do not yet believe that grace sustains them daily.

Galatians 3:3 — Paul addressed this exact condition in the Galatian church: "Are you so foolish? After beginning by means of the Spirit, are you now trying to finish by means of the flesh?" The Galatians had received the gospel of grace. They had been justified freely, through faith. And they had then turned to law-keeping as the mechanism for maintaining and advancing what grace had given them. Paul's response is not gentle: he calls it foolishness. Not because effort is wrong. But because the mechanism of the flesh — trying to earn through performance what can only be received through trust — is the precise opposite of how grace works.

The performance trap produces a specific emotional signature: exhaustion mixed with shame. Exhaustion from the sustained effort of trying to be good enough. Shame from the consistent awareness of falling short. The person in the performance trap does not feel rested. They feel burdened — by the weight of a standard they can never quite meet.

And then Matthew 11:28 arrives: "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest." The rest Jesus offers is not the rest of having finally achieved the standard. It is the rest of having stopped trying to achieve it by the wrong mechanism.

Ephesians 2:8-10 — The Complete Framework

The most complete single statement about grace in the New Testament is Ephesians 2:8-10. It holds together everything that needs to be held together without losing any element.

"For it is by grace you have been saved, through faith — and this is not from yourselves, it is the gift of God — not by works, so that no one can boast. For we are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance for us to do."

Three movements in these three verses:

Movement 1 — Saved by grace through faith, not by works. The source of salvation is grace. The mechanism of receiving it is faith. Neither is earned or achieved — both are given. "This is not from yourselves, it is the gift of God." The faith itself is a gift. Even the act of turning toward God is enabled by the grace that was already reaching toward you.

Movement 2 — Not by works, so that no one can boast. The reason works cannot be the mechanism of salvation is not arbitrary. It is because if they were, boasting would follow — the prideful claim that one's own record merited what was received. Grace eliminates boasting entirely, because there is nothing in the recipient that produced the gift. This is the most radical levelling in human experience: at the foot of the cross, every human being stands in exactly the same condition — needing what cannot be earned.

Movement 3 — Created for good works, which God prepared in advance. This is the verse that is most often neglected in discussions of grace, and it is crucial. Grace does not eliminate works. It repositions them. Works are not the mechanism for earning salvation. They are the natural expression of a life that has received it. They are prepared in advance — not as a list of requirements to complete, but as the life that becomes possible when a person is genuinely living from grace rather than performing toward it.

The person who truly lives from grace — who has received it as a gift rather than straining to earn it — will naturally, freely, joyfully do the good works that flow from that life. Not because they must. Because they can. Because the burden has been lifted and they are finally free to love.

What Grace Does Not Mean

Grace is the most abused word in Christianity in two opposite directions. On one side it is turned into a mechanism of earning — "God gives grace to those who try hard enough." On the other side it is turned into a licence — "grace means God doesn't care what I do."

Romans 6:1-2 addresses the second distortion directly: "Shall we go on sinning so that grace may increase? By no means! We are those who have died to sin; how can we live in it any longer?"

Paul's response to the "grace means anything goes" argument is not to add conditions to grace. It is to point to what grace has actually done: it has united the believer to the death and resurrection of Christ. The person who is genuinely living from grace is a person who has died to the old life and been raised to the new. The freedom grace gives is not freedom to live from the flesh — it is freedom from the compulsion to do so.

Grace does not make sin irrelevant. It makes sin increasingly alien to the person who has genuinely received it. The child who has been fully loved does not use that love as permission to treat their parent with contempt. They are moved by it to respond differently. Grace produces the response that law could never compel.

Receiving Grace — The Practical Step

The person who has spent years in the performance trap does not need new information about grace. They need to receive what they already know.

Hebrews 4:16 — "Let us then approach God's throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need." The throne of grace — not the throne of judgment, not the throne of earned access, not the throne of those who have sufficiently improved. The throne of grace, approached with confidence, by people who need help.

The practical step is not a formula. It is a posture. It is the daily, moment-by-moment choosing to come to God not as someone who has done enough but as someone who is wholly dependent on what has been freely given. It is praying not from a position of achieved standing but from the position of the child who knows they are loved before they say a word.

It is receiving the words of Romans 8:1 as personally, specifically true: "Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." No condemnation. Not: no condemnation for those who have performed well enough this week. No condemnation — full stop — for those who are in Christ.

That is what grace actually means. And receiving it — truly, personally, experientially — changes everything.
