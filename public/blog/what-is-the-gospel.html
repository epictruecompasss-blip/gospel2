THE GOSPEL

What Is the Gospel? The Good News Explained

By In Him Daily  ·  inhimdaily.org

The word "gospel" is everywhere in Christianity. It is in church names, in book titles, in the chorus of nearly every Sunday sermon. And yet if you asked ten different Christians to define it, you would likely receive ten substantially different answers — some about lifestyle transformation, some about God's love in general, some about social justice, some about receiving your breakthrough, some about getting to heaven.

The confusion is real. And it matters — because the gospel is not one Christian concept among many. It is the foundation on which everything else stands. Get it wrong and the whole structure built on it is unstable.

The good news is that Paul defined it. Precisely, carefully, in his own words. And his definition is far simpler and far more specific than most of what is called gospel today.

Paul's Own Definition

1 Corinthians 15 opens with Paul doing something unusual for a letter: he stops and explicitly defines the gospel he preached, reminding the Corinthians of what they had already received.

1 Corinthians 15:1-5 — "Now, brothers and sisters, I want to remind you of the gospel I preached to you, which you received and on which you have taken your stand. By this gospel you are saved, if you hold firmly to the word I preached to you. Otherwise, you have believed in vain. For what I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, and then to the Twelve."

Four elements. Two thousand years have not produced a more concise or more accurate definition.

Christ died for our sins. Not: Christ died as a martyr. Not: Christ died to show us how to live. Christ died for our sins — as the substitutionary sacrifice that the Old Testament sacrificial system had been anticipating for two thousand years. The death was purposeful, directed, aimed at a specific problem: human sin and its consequence.

He was buried. The burial is not incidental. It confirms the reality of the death. Jesus did not swoon, survive, and stage a return. He was buried — which is the ancient world's way of saying: he was dead. Genuinely, verifiably, permanently dead.

He was raised on the third day. The resurrection is not a symbol of ongoing inspiration or a metaphor for spiritual renewal. It is a historical event — Christ's body, the same body that was buried, was raised and transformed. 1 Corinthians 15:20 — Christ was raised from the dead as "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep." A firstfruits is a real sample of the coming harvest, not a symbol of it.

He appeared. Paul lists the appearances: to Peter, to the Twelve, to more than five hundred people at once (most of whom were still alive when Paul wrote and could be questioned), to James, to all the apostles, and finally to Paul himself. The resurrection is grounded in eyewitness testimony, not theological reflection.

This is the gospel. Four facts. One event in three stages. The announcement that something happened — historically, verifiably, publicly — that changes everything for everyone who receives it.

What the Gospel Is Not

Given how widely the word "gospel" is used, it is necessary to say clearly what Paul's definition does not include.

The gospel is not a lifestyle framework. "Live like Jesus" is not the gospel. It is a response to the gospel. The gospel is the announcement of what Christ did. The ethical life that follows from it is the fruit of receiving that announcement, not the announcement itself.

The gospel is not God's general love for humanity. "God loves you and has a wonderful plan for your life" is not the gospel, however true its components may be. The gospel is specific: Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised, was seen. God's love is the motivation behind the gospel — "God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son" — but the love alone is not the announcement. The specific event is the announcement.

The gospel is not a prosperity covenant. The promise that giving money to the right ministry will activate God's blessing on your finances is not the gospel. It is a distortion of the gospel that extracts its language (grace, blessing, faith, seed) while replacing its content (Christ's death and resurrection) with a transactional mechanism. Paul addressed something like this in Galatians 1:6-9 — "I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting the one who called you to live in the grace of Christ and are turning to a different gospel — which is really no gospel at all."

The gospel is not primarily a social programme. Justice, mercy, and care for the poor are genuine implications of the gospel — but they are implications, not the content. The church's social engagement flows from having received the gospel. Replacing the gospel with social engagement is not an advance. It is a subtraction.

The Gospel Is an Announcement, Not an Instruction

This distinction is the most important one to grasp. The gospel is not primarily telling you what to do. It is telling you what has been done.

The Greek word behind "gospel" is euangelion — good news, good announcement. In the first-century Roman world, a euangelion was the announcement of a significant public event — the birth of an heir, a military victory, the accession of a new emperor. It was not advice. It was news. Something happened. This is what has occurred.

When Paul preached the gospel in Thessalonica (Acts 17:1-3), he "reasoned with them from the Scriptures, explaining and proving that the Messiah had to suffer and rise from the dead. This Jesus I am proclaiming to you is the Messiah." He was not teaching them a new way to live. He was announcing an event and its identity — this is the Messiah, and here is what happened to him and why.

The implications of the announcement are enormous. If Christ died for sins, then sin's penalty has been paid — for everyone who receives what was done. If Christ rose from the dead, then death has been defeated — and everyone who is in him will also be raised. The announcement reshapes everything: how the believer relates to God, how they relate to death, how they relate to sin, how they relate to the future.

But the announcement comes first. The implications follow from it.

The Gospel According to the Scriptures

Paul's definition includes a phrase that is easily overlooked: "according to the Scriptures." Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures. He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures.

The death and resurrection of Christ were not surprises. They were the fulfilment of what the entire Old Testament had been building toward. Isaiah 53 described the suffering servant who was "pierced for our transgressions" and "crushed for our iniquities." Psalm 16:10 declared that God would not let his Holy One see decay. Hosea 6:2 anticipated a resurrection on the third day. Jonah's three days in the belly of the great fish was explicitly cited by Jesus as a sign of the Son of Man's three days in the earth (Matthew 12:40).

The gospel is not a New Testament invention. It is the Old Testament promise fulfilled. The law, the prophets, and the psalms — as Jesus himself declared on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:44) — were all pointing toward this event. The gospel is the moment the entire story of Israel arrived at its destination.

This is why the devotional series He Entered Before He Came is not merely an Old Testament commentary. Every chapter of Joshua was pointing toward the one in whose name Joshua was named. And the gospel of Christ's death and resurrection is the event toward which every Old Testament type and shadow was pointing.

Why the Definition Matters

If the gospel is defined vaguely — as love, or transformation, or blessing, or social change — then it becomes whatever the culture most needs at the moment. It can be pulled in any direction. It can be made to mean almost anything.

But if the gospel is what Paul said it was — Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, appeared to witnesses — then it cannot be redefined. It is anchored to history. It either happened or it did not. And if it happened, it has implications that no culture can negotiate away.

Galatians 1:8 — "But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach a gospel other than the one we preached to you, let them be under God's curse." The strongest possible language for the importance of the exact content. The gospel is not flexible. It is not culturally adaptive in its content. It is the announcement of a specific event — and that announcement must not be replaced by anything else, however good or true that other thing may be.
