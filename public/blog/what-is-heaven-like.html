What Is Heaven Like? What the Bible Actually Describes

The popular picture of heaven — clouds, white robes, harps, eternal church service, everyone slightly bored but too polite to say so — has done more damage to genuine Christian hope than almost any false teaching. It has made heaven feel like an obligation rather than a destination, a duty rather than a joy, an eternal continuation of the least engaging parts of Sunday morning rather than the most alive experience imaginable.

The Bible's picture of heaven is nothing like this. And the moment you encounter what the Bible actually describes, the popular picture looks not just inadequate but actively misleading.

This is the most hope-giving article in the In Him Daily blog. Read it slowly.

The First Correction — Heaven Is Not the Destination

The most important correction to make before describing heaven is this: the final destination of the believer in the Bible is not heaven. It is the new creation.

Heaven, in the New Testament, is the intermediate state — where believers are with Christ between physical death and the resurrection of the body. Paul describes it in Philippians 1:23 as "being with Christ, which is better by far." 2 Corinthians 5:8 — "to be away from the body and at home with the Lord." This is real, good, and incomparably better than anything in the present suffering. But it is not the final chapter.

The final chapter is Revelation 21-22. And it is not believers going up to heaven permanently. It is the new Jerusalem coming down — "I saw the Holy City, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God" (Revelation 21:2). The direction of movement in the Bible's climax is not upward and away from the earth. It is downward and into the renewed earth. God comes to dwell with humanity in a creation restored to its intended glory — "God's dwelling place is now among the people, and he will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3).

This matters enormously. The Christian hope is not the escape from the material world into a spiritual realm. It is the redemption of the material world — the transformation of creation itself into the environment where God and his people dwell together without any barrier between them.

The Resurrection Body — Real, Physical, Glorified

The Christian hope for the individual is not the immortality of the soul — the idea that the real you is a spiritual essence that floats free of the body at death and lives on in disembodied bliss. The Christian hope is bodily resurrection.

1 Corinthians 15 is the fullest treatment of the resurrection body in the New Testament. Paul uses the analogy of a seed and a plant: "What you sow does not come to life unless it dies. When you sow, you do not plant the body that will be, but just a seed, perhaps of wheat or of something else. But God gives it a body as he has determined" (15:36-38).

The resurrection body has genuine continuity with the present body — just as the plant is genuinely continuous with the seed from which it grew. But it is also transformed — as different from the present body as a plant is from a seed. 1 Corinthians 15:42-44 describes the contrasts: "The body that is sown is perishable, it is raised imperishable; it is sown in dishonour, it is raised in glory; it is sown in weakness, it is raised in power; it is sown a natural body, it is raised a spiritual body."

The risen Christ gives us the clearest picture of what the resurrection body is like. He was recognisable — Mary recognised him in the garden, the disciples recognised him on the Emmaus road. He ate food — he ate fish with the disciples on the beach (John 21:12-13). He could be touched — Thomas placed his fingers in the wounds (John 20:27). He was genuinely, embodied, physically real. And he was also transformed — he appeared in locked rooms, he ascended, he was changed. The resurrection body is physical but not limited by the physical constraints of the present age.

The New Jerusalem — What Revelation 21-22 Actually Describes

Revelation 21-22 gives the most extended description of the final state in the Bible. It is written in the genre of apocalyptic vision — which means it uses symbolic imagery to describe realities that exceed what ordinary language can contain. But the symbolic imagery points toward realities that are more, not less, substantial than what the symbols describe.

The presence of God — unmediated. Revelation 21:3 — "God's dwelling place is now among the people, and he will dwell with them. They will be his people, and God himself will be with them and be their God." No more temple. No more holy of holies. No veil. No distance. The unhindered, face-to-face presence of God — the thing that the entire history of worship has been reaching toward — is finally, permanently, completely given.

The end of everything wrong. Revelation 21:4 — "He will wipe every tear from their eyes. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the old order of things has passed away." Not less pain. Not pain made sense of. No more pain. Not fewer tears. Every tear wiped. The intimacy of the image — God himself wiping each tear — is as significant as the absence of the tears themselves.

The beauty of the city. The new Jerusalem is described with extraordinary beauty — walls of jasper, foundations of precious stones, gates of single pearls, a street of pure gold "like transparent glass" (21:21). Whether these descriptions are literal or symbolic (and in apocalyptic literature the two are not always separable), they communicate a city of surpassing, overwhelming, almost incomprehensible beauty. The new creation is not grey and abstract. It is vivid, glorious, and specific.

The river and the tree. Revelation 22:1-2 — "the river of the water of life, as clear as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb down the middle of the great street of the city. On each side of the river stood the tree of life, bearing twelve crops of fruit, yielding its fruit every month." The tree of life — present in Eden, lost at the Fall, guarded from re-entry in Genesis 3 — is restored and freely accessible in the new creation. The water of life flows from the throne itself. The image is of abundance, restoration, and access — the garden of Eden amplified to city scale.

The nations healed. Revelation 21:24-26 — "The nations will walk by its light, and the kings of the earth will bring their splendour into it... The glory and honour of the nations will be brought into it." The new creation is not a narrow, ethnically specific destination. The nations bring their glory into it — which biblical scholars have understood as suggesting that the genuine goods of human culture, creativity, and achievement find their fulfilment and transformation in the new creation rather than being discarded.

Will You Know Your Family?

The question that underlies this one is about relationship — specifically, the fear that death is the end of the relationships that have been the most significant part of life on earth.

The New Testament does not give a detailed roadmap of relationship in the resurrection. But the evidence it provides points strongly toward recognition and continuing relationship. The disciples recognised the risen Christ. The Transfiguration (Matthew 17:1-8) shows Moses and Elijah recognisable to the disciples — people who had died centuries before. The parable of Lazarus and the rich man (Luke 16:19-31) depicts recognition across the divide. Paul's consolation in 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 regarding believers who have died is explicitly about reunion — "we who are still alive and are left will be caught up together with them."

Recognition will not be less than what we have known in this life. It will be more — freed from the misunderstandings, the communication failures, the incompleteness that mark all human relationship in the present age, and restored in the fullness that the resurrection body makes possible.

Will It Be Boring?

The fear that heaven will be boring — that eternal life will be an endless repetition of the same experience without the variety and challenge that make earthly life interesting — rests on the assumption that God himself is boring. That assumption cannot survive contact with a God who created fractals, octopi, the Himalayas, music, laughter, and the taste of mango.

Psalm 16:11 — "You make known to me the path of life; you will fill me with joy in your presence, with eternal pleasures at your right hand." Eternal pleasures — the word is plural and unqualified. The presence of God is the most inexhaustible source of interest, beauty, wonder, and delight in existence. A universe that has been exploring his character for fourteen billion years and has not exhausted it will not become boring in eternity.

The new creation is not static. It is a city — with streets, with gates, with the commerce of nations, with the tree bearing twelve crops in an annual rhythm. It has movement, variety, and the ongoing discovery of a God who will always be more than has yet been known. The resurrection body is capable of experiences that the present body cannot access. Eternity will not be too long. It may not be long enough.
